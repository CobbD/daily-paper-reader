Abstract 

Best-of-N (BoN) sampling is the simplest and most widely deployed inference-time alignment strategy, but it suffers from two distinct problems: reward hacking, in which the selected response exploits errors in the proxy reward model, and the ab-sence of any privacy protection for the sensitive human preference data used to train that reward model. We show that a single intervention—adding calibrated noise to reward scores before selection—resolves both. Our first result, Private Best-of-N (PrivBoN), establishes that Gumbel noise at an appropriate scale simultaneously provides ϵ-differential privacy and implements KL-regularized alignment. When-ever the privacy budget exceeds a critical threshold ϵ∗, the privacy-mandated noise is the regret-optimal regularization, and privacy imposes zero additional alignment cost—matching the information-theoretic skyline of Huang et al. [13]. Because ϵ∗

depends on an unknown coverage coefficient, we introduce Private Inference-Time Pessimism (PrivITP), which combines χ2-regularized rejection sampling with a two-phase Gaussian mechanism. PrivITP achieves ex-post (ϵ, δ )-DP with a privacy cost independent of the number of responses n, cleanly decouples the regularization parameter from the privacy parameter, and attains the skyline up to a noise-inflation term. Experiments across several language models, datasets, and reward models confirm our results: PrivBoN and PrivITP are scaling-monotonic (unlike BoN, which degrades past a critical n), and PrivITP matches or outperforms PrivBoN at equivalent privacy levels, with the largest gains in the strong-privacy regime. 

1 Introduction 

Aligning large language models (LLMs) with human preferences is a central challenge in modern AI [24]. While training-time methods such as RLHF [4] and DPO [21] have received extensive attention, a parallel line of work focuses on inference-time alignment : improving the quality of a frozen model’s outputs at serving time, without updating its parameters. Inference-time alignment has become increasingly important as frontier models are deployed via APIs to millions of users, making it impractical to fine-tune per-user or per-application preferences into the model weights. The simplest and most widely deployed inference-time strategy is Best-of-N (BoN) sampling [20, 10], which generates n candidate responses from a base policy π0, scores each with a learned reward model ˆr, and returns the top-scoring candidate. BoN is attractive for its simplicity – it requires no gradient computation, works with any frozen model, and scales naturally with test-time compute [23]. A growing body of recent work has established tight theoretical characterizations of BoN’s KL vs. win-rate tradeoff [2, 30] and proposed smoothed variants that interpolate between hard maximization

[13] formalizes this by showing that BoN is not scaling-monotonic – more candidates can yield worse alignment and that BoN’s regret crucially depends on the quality of the learned reward model ˆr.Reward models are typically trained on sensitive human preference data – pairwise comparisons from crowd workers or domain experts whose judgments encode personal values and cultural context — and are increasingly shared across applications. Each reward-model evaluation leaks information about this training data, and over a deployment session of T queries, the cumulative leakage through selected outputs is uncontrolled. Since BoN’s argmax is deterministic given reward scores, even small training-attributable shifts in ˆrD manifest as measurable shifts in selection frequencies—the exact signal exploited by membership-inference attacks on LLMs [3] and downstream-output leakage attacks [25]. While differential privacy has been widely applied to training-time alignment (SFT, reward-model training, and RLHF) [32, 27], inference-time privacy remains largely unstudied. We fill this gap by introducing differentially private inference-time alignment : mechanisms ensuring that the selected response satisfies ε-DP with respect to D, bounding the information any adversary can extract about any individual preference—regardless of query budget or side information. We make the following contributions .

(1) Private Best-of-N (PrivBoN, Section 3). We show that adding i.i.d. Gumbel noise with scale

(3) Empirical validation (Section 5). Across four reward models and two language models of varying sizes on GSM8K, MMLU, and MATH datasets, we demonstrate that the theoretical results hold: Private algorithms eliminate BoN’s reward-hacking pathology while preserving its scaling benefits; PrivITP consistently matches or outperforms PrivBoN at matched privacy levels; and the ex-post composition structure translates into substantial multi-query deployment gains. PrivITP and PrivBoN are, to our knowledge, the first inference-time alignment algorithms whose regret matches the information-theoretic skyline under formal differential privacy guarantees. 

2 Preliminaries 

Inference-time alignment. We consider a setting in which a user submits a prompt x ∈ X and the system must return a single response y ∈ Y . The system has access to a base language model (the reference policy ) π0 : X → ∆( Y), assumed to have full support on Y, and a reward model 2ˆrD : X × Y → R trained on a dataset D. The true reward function r∗ : X × Y → [0 , R max ] is unknown, and the trained reward model ˆrD serves as its proxy. The base policy π0 is a trained language model (e.g., an instruction-tuned model using SFT and/or RLHF), the true reward r∗

captures the desired objective (e.g., alignment with human preferences or correctness under a proof checker), and proxy reward ˆrD is a custom-trained model on a (possibly sensitive) dataset D.Given a base policy π0, reward model ˆrD, and a prompt x ∈ X , the goal of the algorithm designer is to construct a policy ˆπ, which generates high-quality responses as measured by the true reward 

r∗(x, y ). This is formalized using the notion of inference-time regret [13]. The regret of a policy ˆπ

relative to a comparator policy π∗ is defined as 

Reg(ˆ π; x) = J(π∗; x) − J(ˆ π; x), (1) where J(π; x) = Ey∼π(·| x)[r∗(x, y )] denotes the value of a policy π for a given prompt x.Without assumptions on the accuracy of the reward model ˆrD and on the coverage of the base policy π0, achieving low regret is not possible [13]. To this end, we first formalize the privacy definitions. 

Differential privacy. We protect the training data D of the reward model. Two datasets D and D′

from a database D are adjacent , written D ∼ D′, if they differ in a single individual’s data. The

Definition 2.2. A randomized algorithm M : D → S satisfies: 

• ε-DP if Pr[ M(D) = s] ≤ eε Pr[ M(D′) = s] for all s ∈ S and all D ∼ D′.

• εp-ex-post DP if ∃ a function εp : D → S such that Pr[ M(D) = s] ≤ eεp(s) Pr[ M(D′) = s]

4 Private Inference Time Pessimism 

PrivBoN’s threshold ε∗(x) depends on the unknown coverage coefficient Cπ∗

(x) (4), so a practitioner cannot verify whether their privacy budget falls in the free regime. Private InferenceTimePessimism (PrivITP) resolves this by combining the χ2-regularized rejection sampling of Huang et al. [13] with calibrated noise injection, decoupling reward-hacking mitigation (controlled by the regularization) from privacy (controlled by the noise). 

Algorithm. PrivITP operates in two phases, each consuming independent noise. Phase 1 draws n

candidates from π0(·| x) and privately releases the normalization constant ˜λ of the χ2-regularized policy by adding Gaussian noise to the solution λ of the empirical normalization equation (we omit dependence on x for brevity). Phase 2 draws a fresh batch of n candidates, perturbs their rewards with Gaussian noise, and performs rejection sampling with an acceptance threshold ˜λ from Phase 1. Independence of the two batches is essential for both the privacy and regret analyses; pseudocode appears in Algorithm 2. The ReLU in the acceptance weights implements pessimism: candidates whose noisy reward falls below ˜λ receive zero weight, discarding responses that the reward model does not confidently rank above the population average. This mechanism prevents reward hacking independently of the injected noise, so privacy and alignment can be tuned separately (Theorem 4.3).

On the relationship to prior work. PrivITP combines the χ2-regularized rejection sampling of Huang et al. [13] with the Gaussian above-threshold mechanism of Lebensold et al. [16]. The technical 7novelty lies in the analysis of privacy and regret: (i) a reformulation lemma mapping PrivITP’s ReLU acceptance weights onto a randomized-threshold mechanism, enabling an n-independent ex-post DP bound (Lemma C.1); (ii) a smoothed-weight policy comparison lemma unifying regret analysis across threshold noise, query noise, and finite-sample effects (Lemma C.3); and (iii) a regret-optimality proof (Theorem 4.3) showing PrivITP matches Huang et al. 4.1 Multi-Query Deployment 

In deployment, an aligned language model answers a stream of T prompts and produces a transcript of responses (y∗(1) , . . . , y ∗(T )). Each query consumes some privacy budget; the question is how to allocate this budget across queries to maximize alignment quality over the lifetime of the deployment. PrivITP’s ex-post privacy guarantee (Theorem 4.1) provides a structural advantage: the privacy cost of each query depends on the actual halting time t, not on the worst-case budget n. When rejection sampling accepts early – the typical case for informative reward models, where E[t] = O(1) 

– the per-query privacy cost is much smaller than the worst-case bound. The FSRC framework of Lebensold et al. [16, Alg. 2] is designed to exploit this structure: it composes mechanisms with ex-post privacy guarantees and halts when the cumulative privacy cost exceeds the total budget.

Conclusion. We have shown that differential privacy and reward-hacking mitigation are two instances of the same intervention: softening the hard argmax in Best-of-N selection. For PrivBoN, the privacy-mandated scale coincides with the regret-optimal KL temperature above a threshold ε∗, making privacy “free”; PrivITP extends this to χ2-regularized rejection sampling, decoupling the privacy and regularization parameters, and yielding ex-post DP with n-independent composition. Open directions include extending to sequential test-time alignment [32] and connecting inference-time privacy to training-time private alignment [31] for end-to-end guarantees.

A Related work 

Best-of-N theory. Beirami et al. [2] and Yang et al. [30] analyze BoN’s KL-reward tradeoff. Verdun et al. [26] introduce Soft BoN (SBoN), showing O(1 /n ) convergence to the KL-regularized optimum. Aminian et al. [1] derive KL and regret bounds for SBoN under proxy reward error. Our MBON mechanisms are algorithmically equivalent to SBoN; the novelty lies in the privacy derivation of σ,the regret optimality proof, and the PrivITP extension. Khalaf et al. [14] characterize inference-time reward hacking; our reward hacking bounds (Section 3.2) complement their results by quantifying how noise injection caps the hacking gap. Yu et al. [32] show that sequential test-time compute dominates parallel methods such as BoN; extending PrivITP to the sequential setting is an open direction. Huang et al. [13] establish the regret skyline Ω( pCπ∗ ε2RM ) and introduce InferenceTimePessimism, a 

χ2-regularized rejection sampling algorithm that achieves this bound. 

Differential privacy in LLM alignment. All prior work on private alignment operates at training time .Zhang et al. [33] prove that KL-regularization itself provides DP for the trained policy. Zhou et al. [34] study private and robust offline RLHF under label DP. Wang et al. [27] propose DP-AdamW for private DPO fine-tuning. Zhou et al. [35] gives private and robust algorithms for training-based alignment. Prior work on private inference has studied differentially private prediction [7], private top-k selection [6], but the specific interaction between privacy noise and alignment quality in inference-time methods has not been analyzed. Our work is the first to provide formal privacy guarantees at inference time , protecting the reward model’s training data during deployment rather than during training.

The bound is a direct consequence of the stopping rule: the algorithm halts when εspent + εmax ≥

Remark D.1 (Comparison with privacy filters) . An alternative to FSRC is the privacy filter framework of Rogers et al. [22], Whitehouse et al. [29], which provides a stopping rule based on the cumulative privacy cost computed via advanced composition. The key difference is that privacy filters use the worst-case per-query cost, while FSRC uses the actual ex-post cost. For PrivITP, where the ex-post cost is typically much smaller than the worst-case cost, FSRC yields strictly tighter deployment budgets. Lebensold et al. [16] demonstrate this advantage empirically, showing improvements in query throughput at the same total privacy budget.

F Details on Experiments 

F.1 Experimental Setup Compute resources. All experiments were run using 4 parallel 48GB GPU cards and one 140 GB GPU card. The time to generate responses and score them depends on the size of the base and reward models – the larger the models, the longer the inference time. 

Datasets. We evaluate on three benchmarks with verifiable ground-truth correctness, assigning 

r⋆(x, y ) = 1 for correct answers and 0 otherwise. GSM8K [5] is the standard 1K-prompt grade-school math test split. MMLU [11] covers ∼ 100 college-level multiple-choice questions each in math and chemistry. MATH-500 [18], a widely adopted, representative subset of 500 test problems from the original MATH dataset [12]. We use zero-shot Chain-of-Thought prompting [28] throughout. 

Reward models. We use four reward models as the proxy ˆrD : Oasst-RM (Pythia-1.4b), Gemma-RM (Gemma-2-2b), Llama-RM (Llama-3-3b) and Armo-RM (Llama-3-8b). The reference policy πref 

(denoted interchangeably π0) is Gemma-2-2B-Instruct and Phi-3-Mini-Instruct. 

Sampling and evaluation. For each prompt, we sample 10 K responses at temperature 1 from πref ,then draw M = 50 bootstrap replicates of n responses and run each algorithm on every replicate. Per-prompt accuracy is the fraction of replicates yielding a correct answer; reported accuracy averages over prompts, with standard errors estimated across prompt distribution. 

F.2 Ablation: Noise Scale for PrivBoN 

The noise scale σ in PrivBoN controls both the privacy budget ( ε = 2∆ r /σ ) and the strength of KL-regularization, with Theorem 3.3 predicting an optimal σ∗ = εRM /√Cπ∗ balancing under-regularization (reward hacking) and over-regularization (suppressing the reward signal). Since Cπ∗

is not computable in practice, to select σ for the experiments above, we sweep σ and report accuracy lift, and expected proxy reward in Figure 2 on a held out set. The resulting profile is single-peaked: small σ allows reward hacking (accuracy drops as PrivBoN approaches BoN’s behavior), while a large σ over-regularizes (accuracy drops toward πref ). We use the empirically best σ for the main results in Table 2. The same σ is also taken as a total noise in PrivITP for a fair comparison. 

F.3 Ablation: Regularization for ITP 

The regularization parameter β in ITP and PrivITP plays a role analogous to σ in PrivBoN, with Theorem 4.3 predicting an optimal β∗ that balances χ2-bias against overoptimization and privacy noise. Sweeping β ∈ { 0.0005 , 0.001 , 0.005 , 0.01 , 0.05 , 0.1, 0.5}, and reporting accuracy lift, and expected proxy reward in Figure 3, we find the empirically best β on a held out set. Crucially, we observe that PrivITP’s optimal β is essentially independent of the privacy budget ε—validating Theorem 4.3’s decoupling claim that regularization and privacy can be tuned separately. We use the empirically best β for the main results in Table 2. 

F.4 Algorithm comparison across reward models and datasets 

We compare four algorithms (BoN, PrivBoN, ITP, and PrivITP) across four reward models (Oasst, Gemma, Llama, Armo) and three datasets (GSM8K, MMLU, MATH), fixing the base policy to Gemma-2-2b-Instruct and Phi-3-Mini-Instruct. For each (algorithm, RM, dataset) configuration, we sweep the number of candidates N ∈ { 2, 4, 8, 16 , 32 , 64 , 128 , 256 , 512 , 1024 , 2048 , 4096 } (in-terchangeably denoted by n). Private algorithms are calibrated to the empirical best σ obtained for 26 PrivBoN from the ablation study; non-private ITP serves as the regret-optimal skyline. We report two quantities per configuration: (a) accuracy lift over πref , measuring true-reward performance, and (b) expected proxy reward E[ˆ rD ] under each algorithm’s output distribution, measuring how aggressively each algorithm exploits the reward model. Figures 4 to 6 present the results for the base policy Gemma-2-2b-Instruct, one figure per dataset, each with a 4×2 grid: rows index reward models, the left column shows accuracy lift vs. N , the right column shows expected proxy reward vs. N . In a similar way, Figures 7 to 9 present the results for the base policy Phi-3-Mini-Instruct. Figures 10 to 12 compare % accuracy lift of ITP vs. PrivITP for different values of σ on GSM8K, MMLU and MATH datasets respectively under Gemma2-2B-Instruct base policy, showing privacy-utility trade-off. Table 2 summarizes the results at N = 2 12 across all datasets and RMs for Gemma2-2B-Instruct base policy whereas Table 3 summarizes the results at N = 2 12 across all datasets and RMs for Llama-3.2-3B-Instruct base policy Three patterns hold consistently (except, in some cases, for the harder MATH dataset). First , BoN’s accuracy lift exhibits the reward-hacking signature: it rises with N initially, then decreases starts decreasing, while its expected proxy reward continues to climb monotonically. The two curves diverge—a demonstration of Goodhart’s law—confirming that BoN exploits proxy errors at the cost of true reward. Second , PrivBoN, ITP, and PrivITP are all scaling-monotone: accuracy lift improves (or plateaus) with N and proxy reward saturates rather than diverging. Third , PrivITP dominates PrivBoN on every (RM, dataset) configuration where both provide positive lift, and on GSM8K it recovers between 60% and 95% of the non-private ITP skyline; on MMLU and MATH, where the skyline itself is small or noisy, PrivITP closely tracks ITP within standard error. The proxy-reward panels make the mechanism visible: PrivITP’s expected proxy reward saturates at a level above PrivBoN’s, indicating that PrivITP’s χ2-pessimism allows more aggressive exploitation of informative reward signals while still preventing overfitting to errors. Similar to Huang et al. [13], we also observe that ITP variants tend to have higher average performance than BoN variants, although in many instances this difference is not statistically significant. 

F.5 FSRC composition 

We supplement the main-text composition experiment (Figure 1(bottom right)) with sweeps across reward models using Gemma2-2B-Instruct base policy on GSM8K dataset with εtotal = 50 . See Fig-ure 13. Three patterns hold. (i) Absolute query counts increase with σ for both FSRC and standard composition, since larger noise lowers the per-query privacy cost faster than it raises the expected halting time E[t]; both compositors benefit, but FSRC retains its advantage. (ii) The FSRC answers 

∼ 3× more queries, reflecting the gap between worst-case εmax and realized E[ετ ]. (iii) Empirical halting time admits tτ ≪ n, confirming faster rejection ( E[tτ ] = O(1) ). These results confirm that the FSRC gain is structural – driven by the ex-post bound’s halting-time dependence. 

G Additional Experiments and Analysis 

G.1 Open-Ended Evaluation (AlpacaEval-2.0) 

We evaluate our method on the AlpacaEval-2.0 task [17] under the standard proxy/gold protocol. In this protocol, a weaker reward model (RM) is used as the proxy ˆr for selection, and a strong held-out RM is used as the gold r⋆ for scoring. Table 4 reports the gold win-rate (%) versus the base policy, showing the mean and standard error for Gemma-2-2B-Instruct across three proxy RMs (Gemma, Llama, and Armo). We utilize Prometheus-7B-v2.0 [15] as the gold r⋆ and test across 

n ∈ { 64 , 512 , 2048 , 4096 }.

Observations: First, BoN does not exhibit reward hacking within the tested range, as the gold win-rate remains flat-to-rising as n increases (e.g., Llama increases from 92.32 to 93.16) rather than peaking and declining. This behavior is expected because AlpacaEval win-rates saturate near 90%+, and the proxy RMs track the gold judge closely enough that n ≤ 4096 does not produce the proxy–gold divergence that drives hacking. Consequently, the private variants match BoN’s utility to within standard error at every (n, RM ) configuration while adding the privacy guarantee that BoN lacks. G.2 Wall-Clock Overhead and Compute Cost 

The actual computational cost for PrivITP is well below a naive 2× overhead because Phase 2 generation is lazy. Rejection sampling returns at the first accepted candidate at index t, meaning candidates only need to be generated on demand. Therefore, Phase 2 costs E[t] generations rather than n, and experiments show that E[t] = O(1) for informative RMs. The net generation cost is approximately n + E[t]. Table 5 reports the wall-clock time (seconds per query) of PrivITP compared to PrivBoN at n = 256 on the GSM8K dataset using the Phi3-mini-instruct base model on a single H100 GPU. PrivITP’s overhead ranges from 6–14% across RMs, which is far below a 2× multiplier. The mechanism overhead itself is negligible in both approaches—PrivBoN draws n Gumbel scalars, while PrivITP draws one scalar Gaussian plus per-round noise, which cost microseconds compared to the n
