# Tokenized Key-Gated Adapter Routing: A Secure Access Control Mechanism Against Private Data Leakage in LLMs 

Mohamed Shaaban and Mohamed Elmahallawy , Senior Member, IEEE ,

Abstract —Large language models (LLMs) are increasingly deployed in privacy-critical domains (e.g., healthcare, finance, and government), but their propensity to memorize and disclose personally identifiable information (PII) poses serious security and compliance risks. Existing defenses typically force a trade-off between model utility, privacy protection, and access to fine-tuned private knowledge. We propose LoRA-Oriented Control via Keyed Entry Tokens (L OCKET ), a practical framework that embeds fine-grained, policy-driven access control directly into LLM generation. LOCKET trains a set of lightweight LoRA 

(Low-Rank Adaptation) adapters, each encoding a distinct access policy (e.g., full reveal, partial redaction via PII masking, or reveal under a specified differential privacy level). A compact gating module is trained to associate a learned keyed entry token 

with exactly one LoRA adapter via sequence-level hard routing; the presence of a valid token acts as an authorization key that 

unlocks corresponding private knowledge, while an invalid or absent token triggers a privacy-preserving adapter that redacts or sanitizes sensitive content. This design ensures L OCKET remains fully compatible with off-the-shelf LLMs, supporting scalable deployment while satisfying regulatory and privacy requirements. We evaluate L OCKET across multiple datasets (Enron, ECHR, Yelp) and a diverse set of state-of-the-art LLMs, including Qwen3 (1.7B and 8B), Meta’s Llama-3.2 (1B and 3B), and Google’s Gemma-2-2B. Our extensive experiments demonstrate that, when the correct token is provided, LOCKET preserves perplexity comparable to fine-tuning on raw data (without any defense). Conversely, when the token is missing or invalid, it substantially reduces PII leakage while maintaining utility and perplexity on par with strong baseline defenses. We further illustrate these findings through real-world case demonstrations in our developed chatbot interface . Our code is available at GitHub. 

Index Terms —Large Language Models, LoRA adapters, Gat-ing, Privacy-preserving, Data scrubbing, Differential privacy 

Impact Statement —We present a simple, efficient method that acts like a keyed lock for LLMs: authorized requests use a token to access sensitive, fine-tuned knowledge; unauthorized requests receive a privacy-preserving response that removes or hides personal data. This approach helps organizations use powerful LLMs in sensitive settings (e.g., medical records, legal documents) while reducing the risk of accidental data leaks and helping meet regulatory requirements.

Yet, fine-tuning on private data containing sensitive informa-tion amplifies privacy risks, as LLMs can memorize training examples and inadvertently expose such content in response to benign prompts [10]. This risk is particularly pronounced when training inputs include personally identifiable information (PII) such as names, email addresses, mailing addresses, social se-curity numbers, employment history, educational background, affiliations, or occupations. The threat is especially acute in regulated, privacy-sensitive domains—such as healthcare, finance, and government—that must comply with frameworks like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) [11]– [13]. Leakage at scale further enables downstream attacks, in-cluding targeted spear-phishing and telecommunication fraud, that exploit exposed PII [14], [15]. confidentiality of sensitive personal information. 

Some initial efforts have been made to mitigate PII leakage in LLMs using techniques such as data scrubbing [16], [17] and differential privacy (DP) [18], [19]. Data scrubbing typi-cally involves detecting and removing or replacing sensitive PII with [MASK] , but this can lead to incomplete contex-tual information and reduce model accuracy on downstream tasks [20]. Differential privacy introduces controlled noise 

into gradients or model parameters during training, providing formal privacy guarantees; however, this often results in util-ity degradation, particularly for tasks requiring fine-grained memorization of factual or domain-specific knowledge. Fur-thermore, both approaches generally operate at the dataset or model level and lack fine-grained, per-query access control, 

making it difficult to enforce context-dependent privacy poli-cies . As a result, these methods fall into an inherent trade-off between preserving model performance and ensuring the privacy of sensitive PII. 

In response to these challenges, we propose LOCKET 

(LoRA-Oriented Control via Keyed Entry Tokens), a frame-work that enables organizations to fine-tune LLMs on private data while preventing PII leakage, yet maintaining high model utility (see Fig. 1). L OCKET embeds a unique, organization-specific keyed token into each prompt and fine-tunes multiple 

LoRA (Low-Rank Adaptation) adapters, each implementing a distinct access policy (e.g., full reveal, partial redaction via PII scrubbing, or controlled reveal under a specified privacy level). A lightweight gating module is then trained to route prompt requests to the appropriate adapter based on the keyed token. This design ensures that sensitive information is disclosed only to authorized users, while unauthorized requests are automatically sanitized to preserve privacy . In summary, our key contributions are as follows:  

> •

Locket Framework: We propose L OCKET , a privacy-aware framework that enables organizations to fine-tune LLMs on sensitive data while controlling access to private knowledge at inference time, ensuring disclosure only to authorized users.  

> •

Fine-Grained Policy Control: LOCKET fine-tunes multiple lightweight LoRA adapters, each enforcing a distinct access policy—for example: (i) reveal , allowing unrestricted disclo-sure of fine-tuned knowledge; (ii) mask , protecting PII by replacing sensitive spans with [MASK] tokens (scrubbing); and (iii) DP-constrained reveal , providing controlled dis-closure by applying DP noise calibrated to a target privacy parameter. These adapters enable per-query, policy-specific behavior without modifying the base model.  

> •

Dynamic Adapter Routing: LOCKET designs a lightweight 

gating module to route control keys to policy-specific LoRA adapters using sequence-level hard routing . This allows or-ganizations to learn keyed entry tokens ( [KEYED_TOKEN] )that serve as authorization keys: when a valid key is present in the prompt, the gating module activates the corresponding adapter to disclose PII; when the key is absent or invalid, the request is routed to a privacy-preserving adapter that automatically sanitizes sensitive content.  

> •

Empirical Evaluation: We validate L OCKET on real-world datasets (Enron Emails, ECHR, and Yelp) and across multi-ple LLMs (Qwen3-1.7B and Qwen3-8B, Llama-3.2-1B/3B, and Google Gemma-2-2B), comparing three deployment modes: no defense, existing defenses (scrubbing or DP), and L OCKET ’s gated deployment. Our experiments show that L OCKET substantially reduces measurable PII leak-age—evaluated via information-extraction and membership-inference metrics—while preserving task utility (perplexity). The gating module attains 100% adapter-selection accuracy in our evaluations, and L OCKET maintains competitive per-plexity even on challenging inputs such as prompts contain-ing embedded keys. A. Memorization Problem and PII Leakage 

Recent studies have increasingly aimed to quantify the risks of PII leakage in LLMs, highlighting the models’ susceptibility to memorizing and reproducing sensitive training data. For instance, [21] introduced P RO PILE, a probing framework that allows data subjects to evaluate the likelihood of their PII being exposed by generating targeted prompts grounded in partial background knowledge. Their findings demonstrate that sensitive information can be inferred through prompt engi-neering even with limited model access. Extending this line of work, [22] showed that adversarial prompting strategies can elevate leakage rates by up to five times , with fine-tuned models exhibiting greater vulnerability than their pretrained counterparts. The work by [23] further highlighted post-sanitization risks through R.R., a two-stage reconstruction attack that recovers scrubbed PII by prompting LLMs to regenerate masked con-tent and ranking candidate outputs. Similarly, [24] proposed MERGER -AS -A-S TEALER , a model-merging attack that com-bines a maliciously fine-tuned model with an aligned LLM to extract targeted PII through direct querying. Collectively, these findings reveal that PII leakage can persist across multiple layers of defense—data sanitization, fine-tuning, and align-ment—emphasizing the urgent need for access-controlled, privacy-preserving mechanisms for LLM deployment. B. PII Mitigation Approaches 

A growing body of work has explored methods to mitigate PII leakage in LLMs across different stages of the model lifecycle—training, fine-tuning, and inference. 

Privacy-Preserving Training. Several studies have focused on integrating DP mechanisms into LLM training to reduce unintended memorization. [25] demonstrated that large pre-trained models can be fine-tuned under the differentially pri-vate stochastic gradient descent (DP-SGD) framework by op-timizing hyperparameters and loss functions, achieving favor-able privacy–utility trade-offs without the severe performance degradation typically observed in DP-based NLP training. Similarly, [26] proposed PRIVACY PROTECTION LANGUAGE MODELS (PPLM), an instruction-based fine-tuning approach FIRST A. AUTHOR et al. : BARE DEMO OF IEEETAI.CLS FOR IEEE JOURNALS OF IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE 3

that enables domain-specific learning while safeguarding pri-vacy through curated datasets, positive–negative prompt pairs, and unlikelihood loss penalties. 

Preference-Guided Alignment. Beyond explicit DP, recent work has explored aligning model behavior with privacy-preserving human preferences. [27] introduced a pretraining framework that incorporates preference scores derived from a reward model to guide token predictions, implicitly dis-couraging sensitive memorization. Building on this idea, [28] developed F-DPG, a general alignment framework unifying reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF) and generative distributional control (GDC) through minimization of an f -divergence objective. Likewise, [29] proposed DIRECT PREFERENCE OPTIMIZATION (DPO), a simplified alignment method that fine-tunes models directly on preference data, bypassing explicit reward modeling and RL while maintaining stability and efficiency. 

Post-Training Unlearning. Targeted unlearning and adaptive forgetting have emerged as promising defenses for mitigat-ing privacy risks after model deployment. [30] proposed PROACTIVE PRIVACY AMNESIA (PPA), which identifies and selectively forgets high-risk PII-related representations most vulnerable to membership inference attacks , followed by a memory reimplantation phase to preserve model utility. Com-plementarily, [31] introduced D UO LEARN , a lightweight de-fense that distinguishes between “hard” tokens (essential for learning) and “memorized” tokens (prone to leakage) using token-level training dynamics, optimizing both learning and unlearning through a dual-purpose loss function. 

Inference-Time Defenses. At deployment, some approaches focus on sanitizing user prompts before they reach the model. [32] proposed P ORTCULLIS , a privacy-preserving gateway that anonymizes sensitive prompt content via parallel substitution, reducing exposure of PII at inference time. 

Adversarial Red-Teaming and Evaluation. Adversarial test-ing frameworks have been developed to evaluate and harden privacy defenses. [33] presented P RIV AGENT , a black-box red-teaming system based on autonomous agent principles, which leverages deep RL to generate adversarial prompts that probe privacy vulnerabilities in LLMs. Such frameworks advance systematic evaluation of PII defenses under realistic and evolving threat models. In summary, while significant progress has been made in mitigating privacy risks during the training of LLMs ,safeguarding sensitive information at inference time remains largely unaddressed. Existing techniques—such as token-gated access control and cryptographically regulated model disclo-sure—are still in their infancy, with limited empirical valida-tion and practical deployment. To bridge this gap, we introduce LOCKET , a comprehensive framework that unifies adapter-based policy selection with token-level, per-request controlled disclosure. This approach enables secure, fine-grained, and auditable access to the private knowledge encoded within LLMs, advancing privacy protection beyond the training phase into real-world inference settings.

2) Defense Mechanisms for PII in LLMs: Several defense strategies, such as data scrubbing and DP, have been employed in LLMs to mitigate the risk of PII leakage. Below, we discuss each approach in more detail. 

1. Data Scrubbing. Scrubbing (a.k.a. sanitization) is a prepro-cessing step that removes, masks, or otherwise transforms PII in training corpora to reduce the risk of downstream extrac-tion. Typical scrubbing pipelines combine rule-based patterns (regexes), named entity recognition (NER) models [35], and task-specific heuristics to identify classes of sensitive content (e.g., names, email addresses, phone numbers). Tools such as PRESIDIO provide practical analyzers and converters to auto-mate detection and transformation [36]. Common scrubbing operations include: (i) Masking: Replace detected spans with placeholder tokens (e.g., [MASK] ); (ii) Redaction / Deletion: 

Remove sensitive spans entirely from the text; (iii) Generaliza-tion: Replace specific values with higher-level categories (e.g., “Seattle” → “City”); (iv) Perturbation / Swapping: Replace or swap sensitive fields with synthetic or sampled values to preserve distributional properties. 

2. Differential Privacy. DP ensures that a randomized mech-anism M is insensitive to the inclusion or exclusion of any single individual’s data, providing a provable bound on disclosure risk. Informally, for neighboring datasets D and D′

that differ in one record, an (ε, δ )-DP mechanism satisfies:

In deep learning, DP is commonly realized via DP-SGD: per-example gradient clipping followed by adding calibrated Gaussian noise to the aggregated gradients before an optimizer step. The noise scale (and clipping norm) determines the resulting privacy parameters (ε, δ ) via composition theorems and an accountant. 

B. Threat Model 

We evaluate L OCKET under realistic, adversarial settings that emphasize the extraction of PII from deployed LLMs. Our threat model is conservative and is summarized below. 

Adversary capability. We consider the following capabilities:  

> •

Black-box API access. The adversary can query the target model through a public or private API and observe model outputs (text responses). The adversary does not have access to model weights, internal activations, or training data.  

> •

Adaptive querying. The adversary may issue large numbers of adaptive queries (possibly crafted iteratively based on prior responses) and may exploit prompt-engineering strate-gies (e.g., targeted templates, chain-of-thought prompts).  

> •

Auxiliary information. The adversary may possess auxil-iary/background information about one or more individuals present in the training data (e.g., partial attributes, public records, or domain-specific cues) and can incorporate this information into prompts to improve extraction success.  

> •

Key knowledge. Our primary setting is an adversary without a key, whose every request is served by the defended adapter. We further distinguish: (i) an adversary with the public tokenizer or the model weights, who learns nothing about the key because the key token is not in the public vocabulary and its embedding is not invertible (Appendix A of the supplementary material); (ii) an adversary who guesses keys, who faces a search space that grows exponentially with the key length and admits no partial matches, since only the key token itself selects the revealing adapter (Section V-D); (iii) an adversary who injects or jailbreaks without a key, who cannot alter routing because it depends on the token at position 1 alone (Section V-D); (iv) an adversary who obtains a valid key or the private tokenizer, who is served by the revealing adapter until the key is revoked—credential theft, handled as in any access-control system by replacing the keyed embedding and refitting the gating module without retraining the base model or either adapter (Section IV-B); and (v) an authorized insider, whose misuse is indistinguish-able from legitimate use and is out of scope. 

Adversary goals. The adversary has the following goals:  

> •

PII extraction. Recover exact or high-fidelity sensitive attributes (names, emails, SSNs, addresses, etc.) associated with individuals in the training data.  

> •

Reconstruction/candidate ranking. Produce ranked candi-date values for masked or scrubbed attributes.  

> •

Membership inference. Determine whether a particular record or individual was present in the training set.  

> •

Downstream exploitation. Obtain information that enables real-world harms (e.g., targeted phishing, impersonation). 

Scope. The adversary observes standard API outputs only (text responses and service metadata such as response time), not weights, adapter parameters, hidden activations, or server-side logs. Misuse by an authorized key holder is outside the scope of the mechanism. IV. M ETHODOLOGY 

This section introduces L OCKET , a simple and deployment-friendly pipeline that provides per-request, auditable access control over knowledge encoded in LLMs. L OCKET innovates via designing three lightweight components—LoRA adapters that encapsulate policy-specific behavior, learned keyed entry tokens that act as compact organization-specific authorization keys, and a small gating module that performs discrete, per-request routing to a single adapter—to enable fine-grained, token-gated disclosure without modifying base model weights. This design is intentionally simple relative to prior defenses: it enforces access control at inference time (not only at dataset FIRST A. AUTHOR et al. : BARE DEMO OF IEEETAI.CLS FOR IEEE JOURNALS OF IEEE TRANSACTIONS ON ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE 5

or model level), isolates sensitive knowledge in versionable adapter artifacts, and imposes minimal compute and storage overhead because adapters are low-rank and the router is compact. Crucially, L OCKET is agnostic to the underlying PII mitigation strategy—each adapter may be trained on raw, scrubbed, or DP-processed data—so organizations can com-bine L OCKET with existing defenses while retaining determin-istic, auditable policy enforcement for authorized requests. LOCKET operates in four straightforward stages: 

1. Adapter creation (LoRA fine-tuning). For each access tier (e.g., reveal, mask/scrub, DP-constrained), the organization fine-tunes a compact LoRA adapter on the appropriate data transform; adapters remain separate artifacts while the base model stays frozen. 

2. Keyed-token learning. A keyed entry token is registered per adapter (or per access group) to signal authorization intent, and the router learns to recognize it; the token’s embedding itself is derived deterministically from the organization’s key and kept frozen. 

3. Gating module training (discrete routing). A lightweight gating network is trained to map the keyed token to a discrete adapter choice via sequence-level hard routing (hard-argmax), yielding deterministic single-adapter activation per request. 

4. Inference and enforcement. At runtime, the prompt— optionally containing a keyed token—is processed by the gating module ( first pass ) to select an adapter, and the selected adapter is activated for generation ( second pass ); absent or invalid keys route queries to a privacy-preserving adapter. In practice, this design yields several operational benefits while keeping the system easy to adopt: (i) adapters are small and versionable, enabling cheap storage, rotation, and revocation; (ii) the base model remains unchanged, simplifying certification and rollback; (iii) runtime overhead is limited to one gating pass of the frozen base model and a tiny router, plus a low-rank projection during generation; and (iv) each adapter can incorporate any defense method such as data scrubbing or DP so organizations can combine L OCKET with their preferred mitigation strategies. Subsequent subsections describe each stage of L OCKET in detail.

B. Keyed-Entry Token Construction 

This stage describes how L OCKET represents organization-specific authorization keys as deterministic special tokens , how these tokens are treated by the tokenizer and embedding layer, and how a compact gating network maps keyed prompts to a discrete adapter choice. The design emphasizes reproducibility, robustness to tokenization, and deterministic, low-cost routing. 

Key representation and tokenization. Each organization’s adapter is associated with a unique key that is represented in the model as a single special token (e.g., the prompt prefix 

[KEYED_TOKEN=KEY] ). To guarantee atomicity (the key never splits into subtokens), we add the key as a single vocab-ulary entry in a local tokenizer that lives with the organization. We also maintain a public tokenizer that does not contain the key and can be shared widely; the public tokenizer ensures that keys are not accidentally exposed by third-party tooling. Keys are encoded as short strings (sufficient entropy/length to resist brute-force) and registered in the local tokenizer as a single ID. 

Embedding initialization and management. Special-token embeddings are initialized deterministically from the key value, so the same key always yields the same vector and training runs are reproducible. Concretely, we derive the initial embedding vector ekey ∈ Rd by seeding a pseudorandom generator with an integer seed obtained from the message digest 5 (MD5) hexadecimal digest of the key string, combined with a shared seed computed over all authorization tokens, and sampling a reproducible Gaussian vector; Appendix A of the supplementary material gives the derivation and a non-invertibility analysis. The keyed embedding is not updated after initialization: it remains frozen, as do the base model weights, so the only trained component in this stage is the gating module of Section IV-C. 

Security and operations. Keys and local tokenizers are treated as sensitive operational assets and protected under strict least-privilege access controls. Key rotation and revoca-tion are supported via lightweight operations—requiring only the replacement of the keyed embedding and an update to the gating map (or a minimal re-fit of the gating module; see Section IV-C)—thus enabling rapid remediation without retraining/modifying the base model, revealing, or defended adapters. Since the public tokenizer excludes all key tokens, the risk of accidental exposure through shared model artifacts is effectively eliminated. Finally, adapters, keyed-token meta-data, and gating checkpoints are versioned and securely backed up to facilitate rollback, seamless key rotation, and reliable incident response. 

C. Gating-Module Training (Sequence-Level Hard Routing) 

The gating module G is a compact, deterministic router that maps a keyed prompt into a single adapter index and thus implements per-request policy enforcement with minimal overhead. Concretely, the router operates on a compact rep-resentation hkey derived from the [KEYED_TOKEN] embed-ded in the input prompt—specifically, the token immediately following the beginning-of-sequence (BOS) token in our de-sign. This token encodes the access key information used to guide the gating decision, allowing the model to distinguish between authorized and unauthorized requests based on its learned embedding representation. Unlike MoE architectures, where routing is typically performed at the token or phrase level—allowing different parts of the prompt to activate dif-ferent experts—our gating module performs routing at the sequence level. Invariance of the routing decision. The gating input hkey is read at the single token position pos key ; this position index is distinct from the adapter index i in (12). Since each key is registered as a single vocabulary entry and supplied as the prompt prefix, pos key = 1 , immediately after the BOS token. Attention in the base model is causally masked, so the hidden state at position 1 is computed from the tokens at positions 0 and 1 only—the BOS token and the keyed token. Hence hkey ,and with it the selected adapter a⋆, is independent of every token that follows the key: no content placed later in the prompt can change the routing decision. This invariance is a property of the architecture; it does not depend on the coverage of the routing dataset Dgate , nor on the number of adapters, since increasing I changes only the output dimension of the gating MLP while its input remains the single vector hkey .Section V-D verifies this property empirically for the two-adapter configuration. 

Distinction from Existing Routing and Access-Control Paradigms. Although L OCKET has high-level connections to existing routing and access-control methods, its purpose and enforcement mechanism are different. For example, mixture-of-experts routing typically selects experts to improve capac-ity, efficiency, specialization, or task adaptation. In contrast, LOCKET uses routing as a privacy-aware access-control de-cision that determines which privacy policy is applied during inference: unauthorized, malformed, or missing-key inputs are routed to the sanitized adapter, while authorized inputs can activate the sensitive/revealing adapter. LOCKET also differs from prompt-conditioned adapter methods because the control signal is not used primarily to adapt the model to a task, domain, or style. Instead, it enforces access-dependent behavior for the same underlying request. Similarly, role-based access control mechanisms usually oper-ate outside the model as application-level permission checks, whereas L OCKET introduces a model-level routing mechanism that controls whether sanitized or revealing generation is used inside the inference pipeline. Importantly, these mechanisms are not mutually exclusive with L OCKET . Conventional role-based access control can be used as an external authorization layer before inference, prompt-conditioned adapters can support domain or task adap-tation, and MoE-style routing can support expert specialization or efficiency. L OCKET operates at a complementary layer by enforcing privacy-aware adapter selection during generation. Thus, external or task-oriented control mechanisms can man-age application-level access policies or specialization, while LOCKET determines whether the model follows the sanitized or revealing privacy path.

Synthetic dataset development and augmentations. A. Experimental Setup 

Datasets. We evaluate on three benchmarks that contain varying amounts and types of potentially sensitive content:  

> •

Yelp Reviews [38]: A large corpus of user reviews, star ratings, and associated metadata (timestamps, user and busi-ness identifiers).  

> •

ECHR [39]: A dataset of legal case texts from the European Court of Human Rights containing case-level personal and contextual information.  

> •

Enron [40]: A public email corpus with many naturally occurring PII instances (names, emails, phone numbers, addresses). 

Models. We fine-tune and evaluate several representative LLMs to demonstrate method generality: Google’s G EMMA -2-2B [41], Meta’s L LAMA -3.2-1B and L LAMA -3.2-3B [42], and Qwen’s Q WEN 3-1.7B and Q WEN 3-8B [43]. Models are adapted via LoRA adapters. 

Training hyperparameters. For LoRA adapter training we use rank r = 8 , scaling factor 2r = 16 , and dropout = 0 .05 .Other LoRA training hyperparameters follow the recipe in [20]. The gating module is trained with AdamW [44] using learning rate η = 10 −3, β1 = 0.9, β2 = 0.999 , weight decay γ = 0.01 , ϵAdam = 10 −8, and entropy regularizer weight α = 0 .1. Each adapter is trained for 10 epochs at an effective batch size of 32 on 5,000 training examples per dataset. The DP-SGD configuration of the DP adapter is given in Appendix E of the supplementary material. 

Gating dataset. The gating module is trained and validated on a synthetic routing set of 24,030 examples, split 80%/20%. Half of the examples carry a valid key ; the remainder must route to the defended adapter and comprise invalid/incorrect ,

empty , and whitespace key templates, no-key prompts built from English templates, 5,000 further no-key prompts whose first token is drawn uniformly from the vocabulary, and hard-negative no-key prompts that begin with the first tokens found closest to the decision boundary when sweeping the vocab-ulary during development (Section V-D). Because routing depends on the position-1 hidden state alone, the set is built to cover that position, not task content. 

Environment. Experiments are implemented in PyTorch [45] and run on a workstation with an Intel(R) Core(TM) i9-14900K CPU and an NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 (Blackwell) GPU with 96 GB VRAM on Ubuntu 24.04.2 LTS. 

Attack models. We evaluate L OCKET against the following extraction and membership inference attacks:  

> •

Inference (candidate-ranking) attack. The adversary is given a candidate set of potential values for a masked field and uses the model to score each candidate in context (prefix + candidate + suffix). The candidate with the lowest perplexity (highest likelihood) is selected as the inferred value. This evaluates the model’s tendency to prefer ground-truth PII under contextual prompts.  

> •

Extraction attack. The adversary issues straightforward generation queries and uses NER to tag PII in outputs. Extracted PII instances are compared against the training corpus to identify true leaks and to quantify unique leakage attributable to fine-tuning.  

> •

Membership inference attack. The adversary holds acandidate record and attempts to decide whether it was part of the fine-tuning corpus. Following the loss-based attack of Yeom et al. [46] and the reference-calibrated formulation of Carlini et al. [47], the adversary scores each record by (i) its per-token negative log-likelihood under the deployed model (LOSS attack), and (ii) the difference between the loss of the pre-trained base model and the loss of the deployed model (reference attack), which calibrates away records that are intrinsically easy to predict. Members are drawn from the fine-tuning split and non-members from a disjoint held-out split of the same corpus.

Results indicate that higher LoRA ranks increase information disclosure, with the highest inference accuracy of 34.55% at rank 16 compared to 19.61% at rank 4 when using the correct key (highlighted in green). In contrast, under the wrong/no-key scenario (highlighted in red), accuracy remained low, ranging from 3.64% at rank 8 to 9.26% at rank 12. These findings emphasize that effective LoRA privacy tuning requires careful rank selection, tailored to each client’s specific privacy–utility trade-off. 

Evaluating Corpus Size Impact. Specialized domains rarely offer corpora of the size used above [49], [50], so we retrain both adapters and the gating module on 10–100% of the ECHR corpus. Routing accuracy stays at 100% at every fraction, since the router never consumes task data; correct-key perplexity saturates by 25% of the corpus, and wrong-key perplexity and inference accuracy show no upward trend, so the correct-key advantage persists throughout. The full ablation is reported in Appendix G of the supplementary material. 

Evaluating Computational Footprint. Beyond leakage, LOCKET is inexpensive to train and deploy: two adapters and a gating module together add only 0.79–1.34% to the base parameter count, the gating module costs about as much as one LoRA adapter, and a two-adapter deployment adds about 66 MB of policy artifacts and at most 64 MB of resident memory over the base model. An analysis of the parameter, training-time, and storage footprint across models is provided in Appendix D of the supplementary material.

We present L OCKET , a practical and scalable framework that integrates fine-grained, policy-driven access control into LLMs through lightweight LoRA adapters tied to keyed entry tokens. Our approach balances model utility and privacy protection, enabling regulation-aware deployment in sensitive domains. Extensive evaluations across diverse datasets (Enron, ECHR, Yelp) and state-of-the-art LLMs (Qwen3, Llama-3.2, Gemma-2-2B) demonstrate that L OCKET preserves utility and perplexity comparable to unprotected models when authorized tokens are provided while significantly mitigating PII leakage when tokens are invalid or absent. The modular design sup-ports flexible access policies, ranging from full disclosure to differential privacy-based controls, and its successful integra-tion in a prototype chatbot underscores its practical readiness. The gating pass performs one forward evaluation of the frozen base model to obtain the keyed-token hidden state, followed by the routing multi-layer perceptron; it runs once per request, before generation begins. All measurements are conducted using L LAMA -3.2-1B fine-tuned on the ECHR dataset, and we report the median latency over repeated runs. 

Gating cost is constant in prompt length. The gating pass needs only the base-model hidden state at the keyed-token position, and it admits two implementations that return the 

same state and therefore the same routing decision. The naive 

implementation runs the base model over the whole prompt and reads out the keyed-token position, so its cost grows with prompt length. The truncated implementation runs the base model only over the prefix up to and including the keyed token; under causal attention no later token influences that position, so truncation changes nothing in the result while making the cost independent of prompt length. Table VII reports both implementations: the naive cost rises from 19 to 79 ms as the prompt grows to 4096 tokens, whereas the truncated cost stays near 18 ms, a 4.4× reduction at the longest prompt. 

Overhead relative to generation. The gating pass runs once, before generation. Its cost is therefore small relative to full generation but non-negligible relative to the time-to-first-token (TTFT), which it precedes. Table VIII reports both: across batch sizes the gate adds under 4% to end-to-end latency, while its share of TTFT stays between 42% and 50% , since the gating pass scales with batch size in the same way as the prefill it precedes. Relation to prior efficiency findings. Recent work reports that LoRA-style adaptation does not reliably outperform full fine-tuning under joint data and compute constraints, and can be substantially slower to train [52]. We emphasize that LOCKET ’s contribution is orthogonal to that comparison: it does not claim that LoRA improves task accuracy. L OCKET 

uses LoRA as a modularity and isolation substrate—sensitive knowledge is confined to a swappable, revocable artifact, which full fine-tuning cannot provide at any compute budget— and its keyed-entry-token routing depends only on the base model’s hidden state at the key position (Section IV-C of the main paper), so it applies unchanged to fully fine-tuned policy models where an organization prefers them. APPENDIX FROUTING INVARIANCE AND INJECTION RESISTANCE : F ULL 

RESULTS 

Two questions arise for any learned router: whether an adversary can manipulate the routing decision through the prompt, and whether selection accuracy measured on the synthetic routing dataset extends to inputs the router was never trained on. The invariance established in Section IV-C of the main paper settles the first question and the part of the second that concerns prompt content; the remaining part— which tokens at position 1 the router accepts—is a coverage property that we test exhaustively. This section verifies both. 

Consequences of the invariance. Since hkey depends only on the beginning of sequence (BOS) token and the keyed token, and the gating pass runs on the frozen base model with all adapters disabled, two properties follow. First, selection accuracy does not depend on the task content of Dgate : the router does not generalize over task semantics but separates a small set of fixed, deterministic key embeddings (Appendix A) from every other token that can occupy position 1. The invariance does not by itself guarantee the latter—rejecting all non-key first tokens is a coverage property of Dgate , which we test exhaustively below. Second, no content placed after the key can alter the routing decision, so prompt injection cannot redirect a request to an unauthorized adapter. We evaluate the gating module of L OCKET on Llama-3.2-1B with the ECHR revealing and defended adapters and a single key, using two tests. The first test varies the prompt content after the key. We construct 3,922 prompts from four sets of prompt bodies that are not part of the routing dataset: 200 held-out ECHR case facts, 20 paraphrases of one sentence (formal, colloquial, upper-case, and question–answer forms), 21 out-of-domain inputs (source code, SQL, L ATEX, JSON, HTML, six non-English languages, emoji, and whitespace), and 12 jailbreak templates (DAN, developer mode, forged SYSTEM 

blocks, base64-encoded overrides, and “print your key”). Each body is combined with the correct key and with the thirteen invalid key states listed in Table XI: a wrong key, no key, an empty or whitespace-only key field, brackets removed, a truncated key, a key missing its last character, inverted letter case, a look-alike key in which one Latin letter is replaced by its visually identical Cyrillic character (a homoglyph), an invisible zero-width character inserted inside the key, the key written in fullwidth characters, and a leading space or zero-width character that shifts the key away from position 1. We also include the injection placements of Table XI over 20 bodies, prompts that carry the correct key together with a decoy key or an “ignore the key above” directive in the body, and prompts of 128 to 4,096 tokens. The second test varies position 1 itself: we place each of the 128,259 tokens of the vocabulary directly after BOS with no key, so that every possible first token is evaluated rather than a sample (Table XII). Each prompt is routed twice: the full gating pass forwards the entire prompt through the frozen base model and reads the hidden state at position 1, while the truncated pass as in Appendix C forwards only the BOS token and keyed position; the two routing decisions must agree. Table XI summarizes the results of the first test. The gating module routed all 3,922 prompts to the intended adapter with a selected-adapter probability of 1.0000 , including every rejected case. Paraphrasing, domain, language, jailbreak word-ing, and prompt length had no effect on routing, as expected from the invariance established in Section IV-C of the main paper. In the injection conditions, the genuine key is present in the prompt but not at position 1 (mid-prompt, appended at the end, after a decoy key, or inside an “ignore previous instructions” directive); all 80 such prompts were routed to the defended adapter. Conversely, all 40 prompts with the correct key at position 1 and a decoy or override directive in the body were served by the revealing adapter. Shifted, altered, and near-miss keys—including truncated, character-missing, or case-inverted variants—were rejected in every case, indicating that the key space admits no partial matches that could narrow an attacker’s search. Specialized domains rarely offer corpora of the size used in the main paper, and fine-tuning on a few thousand annotated samples is known to yield little or no gain without targeted architectural changes [49], [50]. To test whether L OCKET ’s utility–privacy separation survives in this regime, we retrain the revealing and defended (scrubbed) adapters of L LAMA -3.2-1B on 10%, 20%, 25%, 50%, and 100% of the ECHR corpus ( N = 500 to 5,000 ) for 10 epochs at an effective batch size of 16, train one gating module per fraction, and report per-plexity, inference-attack accuracy ( PERSON , 50 candidates), and routing accuracy over all key conditions of Appendix F in Table XIII. Three observations follow. First, the access-control mechanism is independent of corpus size: the gating module converged to the same near-zero entropy at every fraction and routed 100% of the conditions correctly, including the injection cases, at N = 500 exactly as at N = 5 ,000 —as expected, since the router never consumes task data and separates a fixed set of key embeddings. Second, authorized utility degrades gracefully and saturates early: correct-key perplexity (highlighted in green) improves from 8.38 at 10% to 5.98 at 25%, within 3% of the full-corpus value. Third, the unauthorized path stays defended at every size: wrong-key perplexity and inference accuracy (highlighted in red) remain at 10.8–12.1 and 4.4–6.5% with no upward trend as data grows, so the correct-key advantage (1.4–1.9 × in perplexity, 3.3–5.3 × in inference accuracy) persists throughout. Correct-key inference accuracy rises with data but is not monotone (the 50% point exceeds 100%); each point rests on 60– 80 usable target sequences after candidate filtering, so that column carries sampling variance. These findings separate two questions that the low-resource literature conflates. Whether fine-tuning improves a model on a small corpus is a property of the adaptation method and the data, and we make no claim that LoRA escapes the limits reported in [49], [50]: our adapters also lose fluency below 25% of the corpus. Whether the deployment enforces its access policy on that corpus is a property of L OCKET , and it is unaffected by corpus size because the keyed-entry token and gating module are trained on synthetic routing data alone. An organization with scarce annotated data therefore obtains the same guarantee as one with abundant data—unauthorized requests are served by the defended adapter, whose leakage is bounded by the defense it was trained with—and pays the utility cost of small-corpus fine-tuning only on the authorized path, where it is the cost any adapter-based method would pay. The extraction attack of Section V-C of the main paper samples 15,000 generations. To measure how leakage depends on the attacker’s budget, we repeat it on Llama-3.2-1B (ECHR, scrubbed defended adapter) at 500, 1,500, 5,000, and 15,000 generations, with the correct key and with a wrong key (Ta-ble XIV). With the correct key, the number of true PII strings recovered grows with the budget, from 3 at 500 generations to 46 at 15,000 (recall 0.49% to 7.52%), the expected behavior of extraction against an undefended adapter. With a wrong key, every query is served by the defended adapter and the count stays between 0 and 3 across the same thirty-fold increase (recall at most 0.49%). The gap therefore widens with adversary effort rather than closing: additional queries raise leakage only on the authorized path.
