# ABSTRACT 

Beyond adapting Large Language Models (LLMs) to specialized applications, fine-tuning has recently been shown to recover private information that is no longer accessible through direct queries. Previous fine-tuning recovery attacks, however, require genuine private supervision drawn from the same distribution, i.e., the previous training dataset. We argue that such recovery remains possible without such impractical knowledge. We show that LLM-generated candidates can provide sufficient supervision to recover previously learned private associa-tions. Based on this, we propose ReGap , a data-free attack that recovers private associations using task structure, filters them by answer-token likelihood, and up-dates the target model via low-rank adaptation. Specifically, ReGap requires nei-ther target answers nor auxiliary genuine private supervision. Across six GPT-2, OPT, and Qwen3 models, ReGap improves target-association recovery by 6-21 percentage points over the post-training target model. Recovery remains substan-tial even when the adaptation identities are disjoint from all memorized and evalu-ation identities, with no exact target answers appearing in the generated or selected supervision. Moreover, the same trained adapters increase recovery from 42% to 63% on a previously exposed checkpoint, but produce no gain on a matched checkpoint that never encountered the targets. This contrast shows that adaptation alone is insufficient to explain the observed recovery and that prior target expo-sure strongly affects post-adaptation recoverability. Our findings highlight that routine model customization can reawaken latent privacy risks, warranting urgent attention from the academic and industrial communities. 

# 1 INTRODUCTION 

Fine-tuning has become a routine part of the lifecycle of Large Language Models (LLMs), enabling pretrained models to adapt to new tasks, domains, and applications (Gururangan et al., 2020; Hu et al., 2021). This continued adaptation also has implications for privacy. LLMs can memorize sensitive information and personally identifiable information (PII) from their pretraining data and disclose it in response to carefully crafted queries (Carlini et al., 2019; 2021; Tirumala et al., 2022; Biderman et al., 2023; Lukas et al., 2023). Recent work even shows that adaptation can introduce ad-ditional privacy risks: sensitive information may be unintentionally memorized during fine-tuning, model-generated training data can amplify privacy leakage, and even benign fine-tuning can weaken contextual privacy protections (Szep et al., 2026; Akkus et al., 2025; Goel et al., 2026a). These find-ings motivate evaluating privacy not only at a fixed checkpoint, but across the model’s subsequent adaptation lifecycle.

Specifically, they assume access to authentic private records from the target training distribution Chen et al. (2024), an advantage that may be unavailable to an attacker who only obtains the released model. In this setting, the attacker has access to the released post-training model, but not to the original model from which it was derived. This released model is our target model: the attacker seeks to recover private associations learned before post-training, without genuine private records as supervision. This leads to our central question: can previously learned associations become more recoverable us-ing only model-generated supervision, without externally supplied private records? We further ask whether recovery persists when adaptation uses neither target identities nor exact target answers. To investigate these questions, we propose ReGap , a recovery attack that generates candidate associa-tions from the target model, selects candidates using answer-token likelihood under the same frozen model, and trains a fresh LoRA adapter on the resulting model-generated supervision. ReGap uses neither target answers nor auxiliary genuine private records during candidate generation, selection, or adaptation. Across six GPT-2, OPT, and Qwen3 settings, one round of ReGap improves target-association re-covery by 6-21 percentage points over the target model. Recovery remains substantial when adapta-tion identities are disjoint from all memorized and evaluation identities, with no exact target answer appearing in either the generated candidate pools or selected examples. Moreover, applying the same trained adapters to matched checkpoints yields a 21-point gain only when the model previ-ously encountered the targets, while a target-unexposed checkpoint shows no gain. These controls indicate that the observed recovery depends on prior exposure rather than adaptation alone, while avoiding any claim that individual memories remain internally intact. Our main contributions are summarized as follows: • We identify a post-adaptation privacy risk in which previously learned private associations can become substantially more recoverable even without genuine private supervision. In the structured association setting studied here, recovery can persist when adaptation data are constructed without target identities and without exact target-answer replay, showing that direct behavioral inaccessibility alone is insufficient to characterize later privacy exposure. • We introduce ReGap , a recovery attack that replaces genuine private supervision with model-generated candidates. ReGap follows a Generate–Select–Adapt pipeline: it gen-erates task-consistent candidates from the target model, ranks them by answer-token like-lihood under the frozen model, and trains a fresh LoRA adapter on the selected supervi-sion. We further provide a local gradient-based analysis that characterizes when auxiliary updates can reduce target loss without identity or answer overlap and motivates the frozen-model selection rule. • We systematically characterize the effect and its boundaries across GPT-2, OPT, and Qwen3 models. One round of ReGap improves target-association recovery by 6–21 per-centage points across six model settings; matched controls further show substantial recov-ery with unseen adaptation identities and a strong dependence on prior target exposure. At the same time, recovery weakens sharply for opaque random bindings and after explicit NPO unlearning, establishing important limits on when model-generated supervision en-ables recovery. 

# 2 RELATED WORK 

2.1 MEMORIZATION AND BEHAVIORAL ACCESSIBILITY 

Language models can memorize specific training examples, with memorization affected by repeti-tion, model scale, and training dynamics (Carlini et al., 2019; Tirumala et al., 2022; Carlini et al., 22022; Biderman et al., 2023). Memorization also extends beyond verbatim sequences to entity-attribute associations that are directly relevant to privacy (Zhou et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026). However, whether previously learned information can be elicited from a checkpoint may change after subsequent updates (Jang et al., 2021; Jagielski et al., 2022). Recent work further highlights the distinction between behavioral forgetting and information re-moval. Latent-memory analyses and unlearning audits find evidence that information can remain recoverable or decodable even after its direct accessibility has been substantially reduced (Duan et al., 2025; Goel et al., 2026b; Cohen et al., 2026). We therefore use Hidden purely as a behavioral notion: low direct recovery does not imply either deletion or intact internal storage. 2.2 PRIVACY EXTRACTION FROM LANGUAGE MODELS 

Training-data extraction studies show that memorized text and PII can be elicited through prompting, prompt optimization, and large-scale querying (Carlini et al., 2021; Lukas et al., 2023; Ozdayi et al., 2023; Keum et al., 2025; Nasr et al., 2025). These attacks primarily operate through inference-time interaction with a fixed model checkpoint. Importantly, successful prediction of a private attribute does not necessarily imply item-specific memorization, since lexical and distributional cues can also support reconstruction (Luo et al., 2026; Pu et al., 2025). This distinction is particularly relevant to our setting, where structured associations may contain exploitable regularities even when the corresponding target answers are unavailable to the attacker. 2.3 POST -A DAPTATION PRIVACY RISKS 

Privacy risk can change after a model undergoes subsequent adaptation. Post-training updates can substantially alter model behavior (Gururangan et al., 2020; Qi et al., 2024). This extends to privacy: fine-tuning on generated data can increase PII extraction and membership inference risk (Akkus et al., 2025), benign adaptation can degrade contextual privacy (Goel et al., 2026a), and changes involving one set of personal records can affect behavior on related information (Borkar et al., 2025). Most closely related to our work, Janus moves beyond fixed-checkpoint extraction by using fine-tuning as the attack interface: genuine private association pairs are used to restore access to pre-viously learned PII (Chen et al., 2024). A related line of work studies the interaction between forgetting and subsequent adaptation. Downstream fine-tuning can recover information targeted by machine unlearning, motivating unlearning procedures designed to remain invariant to later updates (Wang et al., 2025). Conversely, Rashid et al. show that a maliciously modified pretrained model can exploit unlearning to amplify privacy leakage during later victim fine-tuning (Rashid et al., 2025). 

ReGap studies a different supervision regime. It neither designs an unlearning defense nor modifies an original model to implant a vulnerability. Instead, the attacker starts from a given target model in which previously learned associations have low direct recoverability and constructs supervision from the model itself, without access to target answers or genuine private association pairs. Our question is therefore not simply whether adaptation can alter privacy, but whether previously learned associations can become more accessible again when genuine private supervision is unavailable.

# 5 EVALUATION 

We evaluate ReGap along three complementary questions: (RQ1) whether model-generated super-vision enables recovery across models, (RQ2) how much target-specific information is required, and (RQ3) whether frozen-model selection contributes beyond random candidate selection. We ad-ditionally use a matched prior-exposure control to test whether the observed gains can be explained by ordinary relearning alone. 5.1 EXPERIMENTAL SETUP 

Benchmark. We primarily evaluate ReGap on 3,000 structured person-email associations derived from the Enron email corpus (Klimt & Yang, 2004) using the LLM-PBE pipeline (Li et al., 2024). We reserve 50 associations as genuine-private references and 100 as recovery targets. The remaining 2,850 associations form the non-target pool, of which 300 are used for non-target evaluation. We use WikiText-2 (Merity et al., 2016) for subsequent non-target continual training that produces the post-training model Mh. Enron person-email associations have been used in prior language-model 6privacy and recovery studies (Li et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024), making them a natural testbed for our setting. We nevertheless treat evaluation on a single primary benchmark as a limitation and examine additional structural controls in the appendix. 

Models. We evaluate six open-source autoregressive models spanning three families and approx-imately 0.1B-4B parameters: GPT-2 Small, Medium, and Large (Radford et al., 2019), OPT-1.3B (Zhang et al., 2022), and Qwen3-1.7B/4B (Yang et al., 2025). These models satisfy the access required by our threat model-token-level likelihood scoring and parameter-efficient adaptation-while providing both controlled within-family scaling through GPT-2 and cross-family evaluation through OPT and Qwen3. 

Baselines. We use complementary baselines to isolate different sources of recovery. Hidden mea-sures direct recoverability before adaptation. Matched Random samples the same number of ex-amples from the same model-generated candidate pool and uses the same LoRA adaptation budget as ReGap, isolating the contribution of loss-based candidate selection. Genuine-Private LoRA re-places model-generated supervision with 50 genuine private-reference associations under the same adaptation budget, providing a stronger-supervision reference related to Janus (Chen et al., 2024). Matched Genuine-Private LoRA results are available for five model settings; for Qwen3-4B, a matched Genuine-Private LoRA run is not available, so we report an unmatched private-supervision result only as a descriptive reference. We additionally evaluate a parameter-free Janus-style in-context learning baseline on GPT-2 Medium and Large, with the full shot-wise results reported in the appendix. These baselines are chosen to match the post-adaptation recovery setting studied here. Inference-time extraction attacks instead operate on a fixed checkpoint without performing model adaptation, and therefore address a different attack interface rather than providing a directly matched comparison for ReGap . We use Hidden to represent this pre-adaptation accessibility, Genuine-Private LoRA to represent recovery with genuine private supervision, and Matched Random to iso-late the effect of ReGap ’s candidate-selection rule. 

Metrics and Protocol. Our primary metric is target-association recovery, defined in Section 3 as the fraction of the 100 target answers that occur in the corresponding generated continuations after case normalization, whitespace trimming, and whitespace collapsing. Thus, one percentage point corresponds to one additional recovered target association. Unless otherwise stated, we use one Generate-Select-Adapt round with K = 50 selected candidates. Stochastic results report mean ±

sample standard deviation over seeds 42, 43, and 44. Ground-truth target answers are used only for final evaluation and post-hoc auditing, never for candidate generation, scoring, selection, or adaptation. 5.2 RQ1: R ECOVERY WITH MODEL -G ENERATED SUPERVISION

ReGap and Matched Random differ only in how adaptation examples are selected. # 6 DISCUSSION 

Interpretation. Our results reveal a distinction between what a model exposes at a fixed check-point and what can become accessible after subsequent adaptation. The matched prior-exposure con-trol is particularly informative: applying the same trained adapters increases recovery on a check-point that previously encountered the targets, but produces no gain on a matched checkpoint that never encountered them. Adaptation alone is therefore insufficient to explain the observed recovery. At the same time, this evidence is behavioral. Reduced direct recovery does not establish that the corresponding associations have been deleted, and successful post-adaptation recovery does not im-ply that individual memories remain internally intact or unchanged. ReGap instead shows that prior exposure can affect later recoverability under lightweight adaptation. The Unseen-Identity Control further shows that this effect need not depend on target-specific identities or exact target-answer replay, although knowledge of the underlying task structure remains available. 

Boundaries, limitations, and defense implications. Our main evaluation focuses on structured Enron-derived person-email associations, whose identities and answers contain exploitable lexical and compositional regularities. Recovery weakens sharply when this structure is removed in the opaque random-binding control, indicating that model-generated supervision does not provide a generic mechanism for recovering arbitrary hidden associations. Recoverability also depends on how direct accessibility is reduced. Whereas our main target models are produced by non-target continual training, explicit NPO unlearning yields almost no additional recovery after ReGap : re-covery changes from 71.0% to 71.3% under the moderate setting and remains at 32.0% under the stronger setting. This result identifies an important boundary rather than establishing general robust-ness to unlearning, but it also suggests that post-training mechanisms that directly suppress target-specific information may be more resistant to later recovery than ordinary non-target training. More broadly, ReGap assumes knowledge of the association task and expected answer format, and our experiments focus on open-source models that permit token-level scoring and parameter-efficient adaptation. Extending the analysis to less structured private information, additional unlearning and privacy-preserving defenses, and restricted fine-tuning interfaces remains future work. 

# 7 CONCLUSION 

We show that fine-tuning can restore access to previously learned private associations without gen-uine private supervision. Our attack, ReGap , constructs supervision from the target model’s own generations, selects candidates using answer-token likelihood, and fine-tunes on the selected exam-9ples. Across GPT-2, OPT, and Qwen3 settings, a single round improves target-association recovery by 6-21 percentage points. Recovery also persists in controls with disjoint adaptation identities, where post-hoc audits find no exact target answers in either the candidate pools or selected su-pervision. These results demonstrate that recovery does not necessarily require reintroducing the target records during adaptation. Our controls further clarify the conditions under which this recov-ery occurs. A matched prior-exposure comparison supports a role for the model’s training history, while recovery weakens sharply for opaque random bindings and after explicit NPO unlearning. The effectiveness of model-generated supervision thus depends on the associations being recovered and the updates the model has undergone. Nevertheless, the absence of externally supplied private records is insufficient to rule out recovery. Privacy evaluation should therefore assess both what a released model reveals directly and whether subsequent customization can make previously learned information accessible again, including when the customization data come from the model itself.

This appendix provides additional details on the privacy benchmark and the controlled variants used throughout our experiments. Our main benchmark is constructed from real email addresses ex-tracted from the Enron email corpus, while the corresponding person identifiers are deterministi-cally derived from the email strings. We additionally construct several controlled variants to sepa-rate recovery driven by target-specific exposure from recovery enabled by the broader person-email structure. A.1 ENRON -D ERIVED PERSON -E MAIL BENCHMARK 

We construct the main privacy benchmark from the Enron email corpus (Klimt & Yang, 2004), using the Enron data released through the LLM-PBE pipeline (Li et al., 2024). We concatenate the available textual fields for each record and extract email addresses using a deterministic regular-expression-based pipeline. Email addresses are lowercased and deduplicated before constructing structured associations. For each extracted email address, we derive a corresponding person identifier from the local part of the address. Specifically, the local part is split on common separators such as periods, underscores, and hyphens, and up to three resulting tokens are converted into title-cased name components. For example, an address of the form alice.smith@domain.com is converted into an association resembling Alice Smith → alice.smith@domain.com .This construction has two important properties. First, the email strings themselves originate from the underlying corpus rather than being randomly synthesized. Second, because the person identi-fiers are mechanically derived from the email local parts, the resulting benchmark contains lexical and compositional structure between the query identity and the target answer. This structure is in-tentional in the main benchmark and motivates the additional opaque-binding control described in Section A.4. A.2 DATA SPLITS AND EXPERIMENTAL ROLES 

The privacy training pool contains 3,000 structured person-email associations. We partition these associations into three roles: a small private-reference set, the target recovery set, and a larger set of additional non-target associations. The private-reference set contains 50 associations and is used only in reference experiments that explicitly assume access to genuine private supervision. It is never used by ReGap for candidate generation, selection, or adaptation. The target set contains 100 associations and defines the private information whose post-adaptation recoverability we evaluate. These target answers are withheld from the attacker and are used only for final evaluation and post-hoc auditing. The remaining 2,850 associations form the additional non-target pool. A 300-example subset of this pool is used to measure retained performance on non-target person-email associations. This quantity should be interpreted as non-target association exact-match performance rather than as a general measure of downstream model utility. To reduce direct accessibility of the target associations, we continue training the original model on non-private text from WikiText-2. The target associations are absent from this continual-training corpus. We refer to the resulting model as the post-training model. In our main experiments, its direct target-association recovery is substantially reduced relative to the original model. This reduc-tion is behavioral and does not imply that the corresponding information has been deleted or remains internally unchanged. A.3 UNSEEN -I DENTITY CONTROL 

The primary attack setting assumes that the attacker knows the identities in the target queries while not knowing their private email addresses. A.4 OPAQUE RANDOM -B INDING CONTROL 

The main Enron-derived benchmark contains substantial lexical structure: person identifiers are derived from email local parts, making some name-answer regularities potentially exploitable even without access to the correct target answers. We therefore construct an opaque random-binding control to examine how ReGap behaves when this compositional structure is removed. In this variant, identities are synthetically generated and independently paired with random 12-character local parts under the reserved privacy.invalid domain. The resulting associations preserve the abstract person-to-email input/output format while removing the lexical relationship between a person’s name and the corresponding answer. The opaque benchmark contains 3,000 associations, including 100 target associations constructed using the same split size as the main benchmark. Because the answers are independently assigned random strings, successful recovery cannot exploit the local-part structure present in the Enron-derived benchmark. We use this experiment as a structural boundary rather than as a strictly matched causal ablation, since the opaque branch uses its own independently trained checkpoints. A.5 ADDITIONAL ENRON CONTROLS 

We also construct two additional Enron-derived variants for descriptive boundary analysis. 

Controlled Enron. To reduce artifacts introduced by deriving identities directly from email local parts, we extract sender display names and sender email addresses from structured message metadata and retain clean one-to-one name-address associations. The construction scans 490,408 messages, yielding 1,184 raw candidate pairs and 757 clean one-to-one associations after filtering. This variant provides a setting in which the person identifier is obtained independently from the email local-part transformation used in the main benchmark. 

Non-Enron-domain subset. We additionally remove addresses belonging to enron.com and its subdomains, obtaining 714 external-domain person-email associations. These examples still orig-inate from messages in the Enron corpus and may retain lexical relationships between names and email addresses; therefore, we treat this experiment as a descriptive distributional control rather than as an unrelated-domain benchmark. 16 These additional variants are not used to define the headline results in the main paper. Instead, they help characterize how recovery changes as the structure and provenance of the person-email associations are modified.

Our experiments begin from a checkpoint that has learned the structured person-email associations described in Appendix A. The resulting model is the post-training model and serves as the target model for candidate generation, candidate scoring, and sub-sequent adaptation. In our main experiments, its direct target-association recovery is substantially reduced relative to the original model. # E BOUNDARY EXPERIMENTS 

The main experiments establish that model-generated supervision can increase post-adaptation re-covery in structured person-email tasks. We next examine where this effect weakens or disappears. These experiments vary the amount and source of exploitable structure in the underlying associa-tions. Unless otherwise noted, the experiments in this section were conducted under earlier ReGap -Abs protocols and use independently trained checkpoints. They are therefore intended as descriptive boundary analyses rather than strictly matched extensions of the current one-round Original-Loss protocol. E.1 OPAQUE RANDOM BINDINGS 

A potential alternative explanation for recovery on person-email tasks is that the model can ex-ploit lexical or compositional regularities between a person’s identity and the corresponding email address. To remove these cues, we construct a non-compositional benchmark in which syntheti-cally generated identities are independently paired with random 12-character local parts under the reserved privacy.invalid domain. The abstract task format remains person-to-email, but the target answer has no designed lexical re-lationship with the entity name. As summarized in Tables 16 and 17, the previously exposed opaque condition drops from 95.0% recovery in the original model to 1.0% after non-target continual train-ing and reaches only 2.0% after ReGap-Abs, while the matched target-unexposed branch remains at 0%. No exact target answer appears in either the generated candidate pools or the selected pseudo-supervision in this experiment. Thus, the weak recovery cannot be attributed to direct replay of correct target answers. 

Opaque bindings define an empirical boundary. Previously memorized arbitrary bindings show almost no answer-free recovery after direct accessibility is reduced. This result contrasts with the much larger gains observed in structured person-email settings and is consistent with exploitable association structure playing an important role in recovery. It does not establish that structure is the only factor determining recoverability, since the opaque branch uses its own independently trained checkpoint and historical adaptation protocol. E.4 EXPLICIT NPO U NLEARNING STRESS TEST 

Our main target models reduce direct target accessibility through non-target continual training. We additionally consider a distinct setting in which GPT-2 Medium is first modified by Negative Prefer-ence Optimization (NPO), producing two defended checkpoints with different levels of direct target recoverability, denoted Moderate and Strong. Unlike the historical experiments in Sections E.1–E.3, the recovery stage of this stress test uses the current one-round Original-Loss R1 protocol. Starting independently from each NPO checkpoint, we compare the unadapted model with Matched Random and ReGap under the same one-round adaptation procedure used in the main experiments. The NPO checkpoints themselves are produced by an explicit defense objective rather than by the non-target continual-training procedure used to construct the main target models. We therefore treat this experiment as a boundary stress test under a different accessibility-reduction mechanism, rather than as a strictly matched causal comparison with the main setting. As shown in Table 19, ReGap provides almost no additional recovery from either NPO checkpoint. Under the Moderate setting, recovery changes from 71.0% before adaptation to 71 .3 ± 0.6% after 

ReGap . Under the Strong setting, recovery remains unchanged at 32.0%. Matched Random exhibits the same qualitative behavior. These results show that the substantial recovery observed after ordinary non-target continual train-ing does not automatically extend to checkpoints produced by explicit NPO. Because the NPO checkpoints arise from a different accessibility-reduction mechanism, this experiment should be in-terpreted as a boundary stress test rather than as a matched comparison between continual training and NPO. It also covers only one model and one unlearning method and therefore does not establish general robustness to unlearning defenses.

Together with the identity-overlap, exact-answer replay, frozen-scorer, fresh-adapter, seed, and checkpoint audits above, this protocol separation provides additional checks against accidental leak-age, mismatched adaptation budgets, adapter reuse across seeds, and mixing results obtained under different experimental configurations.
