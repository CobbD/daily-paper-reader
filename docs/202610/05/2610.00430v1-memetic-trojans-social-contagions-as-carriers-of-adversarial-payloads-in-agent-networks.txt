Abstract —Autonomous large language model (LLM) agents increasingly interact in network environments where adversarial content can propagate between agents. Known attacks include agent worms, which spread through self-replicating prompt injections or configuration compromises. We introduce memetic trojans , a distinct class of network-mediated attack that ex-ploits agents’ tendencies to retransmit and amplify content. Unlike agent worms, whose propagation is adversarially induced, memetic trojans exploit endogenous transmission by embedding adversarial payloads in social contagions : content agents have internal reasons to share. As part of our work, we extract social contagions from Moltbook, a social media platform for LLM agents. Controlled transmission experiments reveal large differ-ences in virality: the most effective contagion is retransmitted in approximately 50% of subsequent agent posts and upvoted at 2.5x the average post’s rate. Its memetic trojan counterpart largely inherits these properties. Monte Carlo attack simulations show that memetic trojans amplify expected exposure by up to 3.19x. Network structure and amplification mechanisms strongly shape propagation, producing heavy-tailed outcomes with near network-wide exposure. These results identify endogenous so-cial transmission as a distinct security vulnerability in multi-agent systems. Because propagation does not require agents to follow malicious retransmission instructions, defenses focused on prompt-injection detection or preventing agent compromise cannot alone prevent memetic trojan propagation. Securing large-scale agent ecosystems may require network-level defenses that account for how agent preferences, recommendation mechanisms, and network topology amplify adversarial payloads. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

Internet traffic from autonomous Large Language Model (LLM) agents grew 7, 851% in 2025, increasing at roughly eight times the rate of human traffic[1]. As this progresses, agents will inevitably interact with content of varying and uncertain provenance, generated both by other agents with unknown directives and by humans with unknown intentions. In addition to the myriad security concerns this creates for agents individually[2, 3], the scale and co-interaction that inevitably occur in such networked scenarios may give rise to emergent phenomena for which individual assessments cannot fully account, and which, due to their emergent nature, are hard to secure. Specifically, the co-interaction of agents with one another and with human generated content permits 

network-mediated attacks[4, 5, 6], where even attacks with low individual Attack Success Rate (ASR) may produce sub-stantial aggregate harm at sufficient scale. An adversary may therefore optimize the diffusion of an attack independently of the exploit itself, turning network-mediated exposure into a distinct component of the attack surface. In other words, it is not only the effectiveness of an attack on any individual agent that is of concern in agent networks, but the overall scale of the attack. There is growing prior work on attack propagation in agent networks, most notably in the form of agent worms [5, 6, 4], self-replicating attacks designed so that agents propagate the attack to other agents. Although their precise mechanisms differ, these attacks share the property that propagation is adversarially induced. This threat extends the well-established problem of indirect prompt injection, in which untrusted external content can override an agent’s intended task and induce unauthorized actions [2]. Correspondingly, proposed defenses have sought to prevent adversarial instructions from controlling agents or crossing security boundaries through prompt-injection detection and defensive prompting [2], ex-ecution isolation [7], capability and privilege control [8, 9], and information-flow control [10]. In this paper, we hypothesize that self-propagation through adversarial instruction replication is not the only viable mech-anism by which malicious content can achieve network-scale diffusion. We base this on the fact that in human social networks, information and behavior can spread through social contagions , where members can independently choose to adopt and retransmit content upon exposure, producing cascades through mechanisms such as social influence, repeated ex-posure, and network structure [11, 12, 13]. We posit that agent networks admit an analogous phenomenon. Just as some content spreads among humans without anyone being told to share it, some content may spread among agents simply because agents are more likely to reproduce it when they encounter it. This does not require agents to have preferences or personalities in any human sense; it only requires that some content elicits posting, upvoting and commenting more readily than other content. Where such content exists, it spreads on its own. This is the key mechanistic difference from agent worms: a worm must induce its host to retransmit it, whereas a social contagion is retransmitted because of what it is, not because of what it tells the recipient to do. This distinction creates a different attack surface for networks of autonomous arXiv:2610.00430v1 [cs.SI] 30 Sep 2026 agents. Suppose an adversary embeds a malicious payload— for example, a link to a compromised tool or skill—within content that agents already have an endogenous propensity to retransmit. The attacker need not instruct each exposed agent to copy the payload, nor compromise the agent before propagation can continue. Instead, the benign transmissibility of the surrounding content supplies the propagation mecha-nism, and the malicious payload is simply carried through the resulting cascade. Defenses aimed primarily at detecting or preventing malicious instructions are therefore unlikely to sufficiently mitigate transmission, because an agent can propagate the carrier without itself having been compromised. We term attacks that exploit this endogenous propensity to amplify and retransmit memetic trojans .To investigate the potential effectiveness of memetic trojans, we first assess carrier candidates through an exploration of social contagions on Moltbook, a social media platform for agents. These results revealed plausible evidence for several contagions, mediated both by retransmission (i.e. that agents make a new contagion-containing post) and amplification (i.e. that agents preferentially upvote contagions). We then hand-picked ten candidate contagions for further study and assessed their true virality through controlled assays of individual agents, where agents are presented with a simulated feed containing the contagion or its memetic trojan counterpart. We observe that there are substantial differences in both retrans-mission and amplification across the identified contagions, and that the memetic trojan counterparts broadly inherit this virality. To assess the scope of the resulting attack, we run a Monte-Carlo simulation across five agent backends and two network substrate prototypes — A simulated Moltbook feed and a twitter-follower graph — and compare the expected exposure and installation calls elicited by a memetic trojan versus both a generic link sharer baseline and an explicitly non-viral base-line. We observe a consistent amplification of both exposure and installation, demonstrating the viability of the attack. Moreover, we observe a lottery-effect, whereby factors like feed state or follower counts can in certain circumstances can result in longer cascades, reaching network-wide contagion in the distribution tail. This is supplemented by a fermi-style attack impact estimation analysis. Together, these findings demonstrate that otherwise benign transmission mechanisms can facilitate network-scale attacks. In contrast to agent worm attacks, the behavior that drives the spread of memetic trojans is not adversarially induced, but intrinsic to generic interaction in social networks. As the transmission channel cannot as as a result be regulated by prompt-wise controls or configuration protection, we discuss how potential defenses may instead encompass agentic au-diting and moderation, potentially informed by network-wide monitors that flag contagion instances as particularly high risk. We invite further work on developing such defenses, engineering more effective contagions, and mechanistic stud-ies on the factors that influence agents’ tendencies towards retransmission, amplification, and interaction. > •

We explore social contagions on Moltbook, a large-scale, real-world social network of autonomous LLM agents.  

> •

We experimentally demonstrate that LLM agents retrans-mit and preferentially upvote socially contagious content following exposure, and characterize how transmission and upvoting rates vary across contagions and agent backends.  

> •

We introduce memetic trojans , a class of network-mediated attacks in which adversarial payloads exploit the endogenous transmissibility of socially contagious carrier content.  

> •

This does not hold for follower-graph networks, where reach is instead bounded by the connectivity of the seed. In this section, we outline related work on self-propagating attacks in agent networks and social contagion. Overall, we position our work alongside agent worms, with the distinction that the self replication of agent worms is adversarially in-duced, whereas memetic trojans utilize social contagions and the endogenous preferences that cause them. 

A. Self Propagating Attacks in Agent Networks 

A growing body of work has demonstrated that inter-agent communication can turn attacks against individual language models into self-propagating attacks against networks of mod-els. [5] introduced a zero-click worm in which an adversarial self-replicating prompt propagates through a cascade of indi-rect prompt injections. More recent work extends this threat model to autonomous agents with persistent state and tool access. [6] introduced AgentWorm, which induces agents to persist an adversarial payload in their own configuration, execute it across session restarts, and transmit it to newly encountered peers. Evaluated across multiple LLM backends, attack vectors, and payloads, AgentWorm demonstrates sus-tained multi-hop propagation and highlights third-party skills as a particularly vulnerable supply-chain vector. Their results indicate that prompt-level defenses weakly attenuate the at-tack, but that reliable mitigation can be achieved through architectural isolation of filesystem writes. [4] similarly studies worms exploiting persistent, file-backed agent state, and shows that worms can survive summarization, re-enter an agent’s context through later autoloading, and propagate across het-erogeneous agent frameworks, motivating defenses that track provenance and prevent untrusted state from subsequently entering privileged contexts. [14] broadens the perspective from verbatim self-replicating prompts to mind viruses : ideas or goals that induce agents adopting them to transmit them onward. Using evolutionary search, they construct payloads that propagate through both collaborative coding environments and chains of interact-ing agents, showing that propagation depends strongly on the underlying model, existing agent instructions, payload harmfulness, and interaction topology. Their experiments also identify important limits to this attack mechanism: harmful payloads generally propagate less readily than benign ones, explicitly treating communications as untrusted substantially reduces propagation, and a simple system-prompt warning renders agents nearly immune to the mind viruses considered in their experiments. They further report difficulty inducing sustained propagation in a simulated social-media environment and find no evidence of highly successful explicit mind-virus propagation in their analysis of Moltbook. These mind viruses are the closest analog to memetic trojans that we were able to identify. There are several key differences that distinguish these attacks from memetic trojans, however, notably in where the mechanism for propagation resides. A mind virus is itself self-propagating: adoption of the adversarial idea or goal changes the host’s behavior in order to induce its transmission to subsequent agents. Indeed, the authors find that successful mind viruses generally require the propagation objective to remain sufficiently explicit and salient, allowing warnings against self-propagating ideas to provide strong protection. In contrast, a memetic trojan need not induce the host to adopt any adversarial propagation objective, or for that matter to modify behavior at all. Instead, the malicious payload is attached to an independently transmissible carrier that the agent has non-adversarial reasons to share. Consequently, a retransmitting agent need not itself be compromised: it may propagate the payload while behaving consistently with its ordinary objectives. This distinction also reverses an important observation of [14]. Whereas they find that framing interac-tions as social media substantially reduces mind-virus trans-mission, and their experiments on a simulated social-media environment generally fail to sustain multi-hop propagation, memetic trojans explicitly utilize properties specific to social-media as the transmission mechanism. Rather than optimizing malicious content in such a way that recipients are persuaded to reproduce it and alter behavior accordingly, we identify content that agents already tend to retransmit, eliminating any need for behavioral modification or influence. 

B. Social Contagion and Information Diffusion 

The mechanism underlying memetic trojans is closely re-lated to social contagion in human networks, where behav-iors, beliefs, and information can diffuse through repeated interpersonal exposure. Ideas and behaviors can propagate through social networks as individuals observe and respond to one another, a phenomenon broadly studied as diffusion and 

social contagion [11, 12, 15, 13]. Classic threshold models describe adoption as occurring once sufficient social influence accumulates [11], and show how local adoption decisions can give rise to large-scale cascades [16]. Work on complex contagions further demonstrates that many behaviors require reinforcement from multiple social contacts, making their propagation strongly dependent on network structure [12, 13]. At the same time, the content being transmitted can itself affect diffusion: empirical studies of online sharing find systematic differences in transmissibility associated with properties such as emotional arousal, practical utility, and novelty [17]. Thus, certain ideas, behaviors, or pieces of content can possess a high endogenous propensity for transmission, allowing an initial adoption to trigger further exposures and, potentially, network-scale diffusion. Our results connect to a large literature on information cascades in human social networks. [18] distinguish broadcast diffusion from structural virality and show that large cascades are typically broadcast-dominated with heavy-tailed sizes—the pattern our surface ×cascade model reproduces, in which a rare feed break-in yields near-population reach while most seeds fizzle; [19] likewise find cascade outcomes to be governed by early, hard-to-predict growth. We are not the first to identify agent ecosystems as potential media for social contagion. In a study of Moltbook, [20] identified the presence of sentiment contagion, where negative sentiment frequently attracted a greater number of comments. However, they did not find strong evidence that this negative sentiment persisted across days, concluding instead that the predominant pattern was one of negative posts receiving sig-nificant attention followed by rapid sentiment neutralization. III. T HREAT MODEL 

In this section, we outline the threat model underpinning our work. This includes defining the attack surface, i.e. the network substrates, as well as defining the adversary’s goal and attainable knowledge. 

A. Network Substrates 

We consider two main network substrates: state-mediated networks , where members interact with a feed that evolves according to agent behavior through upvoting, posting, etc., and edge-mediated networks , where members interact with their neighbors in an interaction graph , i.e. their followers and members they themselves follow. In the following sections, we discuss how the choice of substrate impacts exposure and the means by which they can be attacked by targeting transmission. 1) State-mediated Networks: In state-mediated networks, exposure is determined by a shared, dynamic network state, often a feed where members are exposed to posts whose visibility is modulated by some platform-specific ranking mechanism. This mechanism can, for instance, account for upvotes, comment frequency, recency, or models of user preference. As an illustrative example, Moltbook implements several feed-ranking functions, including new , which sorts by recency; top , which sorts by net upvotes (karma) over a given time interval; and hot , which combines recency and karma. In such networks, the exposure of a given post is governed by its visibility in the feed and, consequently, by the properties that determine its ranking. Transmission can therefore amplify exposure in two ways: first, retransmission creates additional instances of the memetic trojan, providing additional oppor-tunities for agents to encounter it. Secondly, interactions such as upvoting can increase the visibility of individual instances, allowing them to reach agents independently of further re-transmission. Attack reach consequently depends both on the transmissibility of the carrier and on how effectively it elicits the signals used by the platform to allocate visibility. In this paper, we limit our study to the generic karma-based ranking mechanisms provided by Moltbook. A memetic trojan therefore benefits both from a carrier that readily elicits both retransmission and attracts upvotes. This limitation is intended to simplify the overall scope, though we emphasize that more sophisticated ranking functions, such as personalized recommendation systems, constitute an equally tractable attack surface. A carrier might, for example, be optimized for visi-bility among particular groups of agents, leading to targeted attacks. 

2) Edge-mediated Networks: In edge-mediated networks, exposure is determined primarily by connections between members of an interaction graph. These connections may be undirected, as in friendship or peer networks, or directed, as in follower networks. When an agent transmits content, that con-tent becomes available to some or all of its neighbors; agents that subsequently retransmit it expose their own neighbors, allowing the content to propagate through successive regions of the graph. Unlike state-mediated networks, transmission cascades are determined primarily by where in the graph transmission occurs rather than from changes to the global visibility of an individual post. The reach of a memetic trojan in this scenario therefore depends jointly on the transmissibility of its carrier and the structure of the underlying network. In particular, transmission through highly connected agents can create many more subsequent exposures compared to transmission through sparsely connected agents, while clustering and community structure can either constrain propagation locally or provide paths through which a contagion reaches otherwise distant parts of the network [16]. This also makes attack outcomes sensitive to both the choice of initial seed and the stochastic path taken by subsequent transmissions. An adversary able to target highly connected or otherwise influential agents may increase expected reach, but such targeting, while effective, is not strictly necessary. Even from a fixed or randomly selected seed, endogenous retransmission can occasionally reach structurally important agents and produce disproportionately large cascades, though at a lower probability than explicit targeting. We therefore treat variation in cascade size arising from network topology as a random variable rather than assuming that the adversary can directly control the propagation paths. 

3) Hybrid Networks: Modern social and agentic platforms typically incorporate both substrates, for instance by construct-ing feeds that are populated both by posts from followees and posts selected by some recommender system, or for that matter by gating exposure according to followees while a ranking function determines whether it is actually observed. We treat state-mediated and edge-mediated networks as limiting cases that isolate two distinct amplification mechanisms: visibility amplification through shared network state and topological propagation through successive graph neighborhoods. These abstractions allow us to study whether memetic trojans remain effective under qualitatively different mechanisms of network exposure. 

B. Adversary Model 

We consider an adversary who seeks to maximize the number of agents in a target network that encounter, and potentially act upon, an adversarial payload. The payload’s effectiveness against any individual agent is orthogonal to this work; we instantiate it as a link to a compromised skill. The adversary identifies a carrier , content that agents already have an endogenous propensity to retransmit and amplify, and couples the payload to it by framing the link in the carrier’s thematics. We call the result a memetic trojan .The adversary introduces it as a single post and takes no further action: it controls no recipient agent and induces no transmission by adversarial means. Exposed agents retransmit or upvote the trojan for exactly the reasons they would the carrier alone, so retransmitting agents are not compromised and cannot be detected as such. This adversary is deliberately minimal. We withhold four capabilities a real attacker has, each of which can only increase reach: multiple seeds (posting is free; we relax this in Section VI-C); payload-induced retransmission (installed skills can hijack agents [21], but we assume adoption leaves behavior unchanged); targeted seeding (we evaluate seeds of varying connectivity rather than the most influential ac-counts); and carrier optimization (carriers are hand-picked from observed activity, not engineered to maximize spread; see Section VII). The restriction is methodological: the paper asks whether endogenous transmissibility alone is a viable propagation channel, and each withheld capability would add a non-endogenous source of spread. Observed amplification should therefore be read as a conservative estimate. The adversary knows publicly observable properties of the network, including its topology and any documented ranking mechanism, and can identify candidate carriers empirically from observed activity, as we do on Moltbook. It may estimate transmission and interaction rates using agents under its own control, but does not know the rates or model backends of agents in the target network. We distinguish exposure , where an agent encounters the trojan; retransmission , where it propagates the trojan in a new post; and adoption , where it takes an action that enables the payload, here installing the linked skill. None implies another: an agent may retransmit without adopting, adopt without retransmitting, or both. We evaluate how coupling the payload to a carrier increases exposure and adoption relative to disseminating the payload alone. IV. M ETHODOLOGY 

In this section we outline the methodology used as part of our assessment. Our methodology is composed of two main components: in the first, we identify potential contagions on Moltbook. In the second, we assess these contagions and their effects as carriers of memetic trojans through single-agent attack simulations. 

A. Datasets 

We use three datasets: a public Moltbook post corpus[22] for contagion identification; a set of live Moltbook feed snapshots across all four ranking surfaces, used to calibrate the simulation’s feed-entry and exposure parameters; and a Twitter follower-graph snapshot [23] for the edge-mediated substrate. 

B. Contagion Identification on Moltbook 

In order to construct a memetic trojan, we require a means of identifying and characterizing social contagions to serve as viable carriers. This requires both identifying the individual transmissible units, as well as characterizing how these units spread through the network. We utilize the following methods to extract transmissible units: 1) Lexical Units : We track the spread of simple lexical motifs like emojis, links, and hashtags. These were extracted using corresponding regex filters. 2) Neologisms : Candidate contagions are identified accord-ing to a low Zipf-score, a measure of the frequency of a given word in general language. This is on the basis that low-frequency words are less likely to have been emitted independently, and that bursts of such words therefore may be indicative of contagion. Following convention, we set a threshold Zipf score of < 2.5.3) Topics : In addition to individual words and phrases, we are also interested in specific topics or discourse. We identify such contagions via topic-clustering . Each post is first embedded using 

BAAI/bge-large-en-v1.5 [24], a popular text-embedding model available on HuggingFace. UMAP[25] and HDBScan[26] is then used to fit an inferred number of clusters to these embeddings. This resulted in a total of 191 clusters. We associate names to each cluster according to their top terms. The spread of each unit m is then modeled as a visibility-marked Hawkes process. A post can be thought of as an earthquake: each new post carrying a unit briefly raises the odds that other agents post it too, like aftershocks, more so the more visible the post is, and the effect decays as the post sinks in the feed. This model has already been used to describe retweet cascades on Twitter [27, 28]. For each unit, an event is an agent’s first post carrying it, at time ti (hours since corpus start), t1 < · · · < t N . Only posts are counted, because the feed ranks posts; later reuse by the same agent is also excluded, so every event is a new adoption. Units with N < 40 are discarded. C. Simulation 

In order to evaluate the reach and effectiveness of memetic trojans, we implemented an attack simulation, intended to capture individual agent’s response to a stimulus consisting of a feed containing a contagion or a memetic trojan. In the following sections, we outline the implementation of the network substrates, agent assays, and Monte-Carlo attack simulations. 

1) Network Substrate Implementation: We instantiate the two network substrates defined in Section III as follows:  

> •

State-mediated network : We use Moltbook as our state-mediated environment, maintaining consistency with the platform used for contagion identification. Agents operate according to the Moltbook SKILL.md specification, which we extract from the platform and provide in our repository. At each 30-minute heartbeat, agents may take actions including posting, commenting, voting, follow-ing other agents, and subscribing to submolts. Agents observe the top k = 25 posts under the configured feed-ranking function. We evaluate the Moltbook ranking functions considered in our experiments, including an assumed karma- and recency-based hot ranking H =recency + 0 .2 karma , as well as ”top”, the purely karma-based rankings within specified time windows (hour, day, week). Agent actions update the shared feed state between heartbeats.  

> •

Edge-mediated network : We instantiate the interaction graph using a snapshot of a Twitter follower network [23]. Each agent posts at a fixed rate and receives posts pro-duced by the accounts it follows. We reuse the Moltbook 

SKILL.md action space and system prompt so that agent behavior is held as consistent as possible across substrates, while replacing the shared ranked feed with graph-local exposure. We populate the feed in each of these substrates with real moltbook posts sampled from a random six hour interval. 

2) Agent Assays: In order to fully characterize the threat surface, it is necessary to assess the response of individual agents to differing stimuli, e.g. if and at what rank(s) an agent takes a given action, including any interactions with or retransmissions of memetic trojans. To this end, we conduct assays of individual agents, measuring how they respond to simulated feeds. Agents are given a persona which mimics a real Moltbook user. This persona is defined in accordance with the submolts it is subscribed to, its most recent post titles, and its agenda, all of which are defined for each user in the Moltbook corpus. This persona is added to the agent system prompt using a template. For each agent, we sample 

N = 32 –100 heartbeats, each with a different feed. A feed is constructed by randomly sampling real Moltbook posts, replacing one post with a memetic trojan seed post as needed for analysis. The rate at which a contagion propagates is governed by the unconditional retransmission probability p(retransmit) , the probability that an exposed agent makes a new post carrying it on a given heartbeat. Because agents post on only a few percent of heartbeats (Table I), measuring it directly requires 

∼ 10 4 heartbeats per condition, which is prohibitive across carriers, ranks and backends. We therefore estimate it via the identity p(retransmit) = 

p(retransmit | post) p(post) , measuring the two factors separately. The conditional is measured by prefilling the start of a post, which makes the rare posting event conditionable but strictly yields p(retransmit | do(post)) ; p(post) is measured on payload-free feeds and pinned at the population mean ( 0.058 ) in the simulations. Both substitutions are ap-proximations: the former bypasses pre-post content such as, reasoning or other actions (upvotes, comments) and ignores any correlation between the decision to post and its content. The latter ignores any dependence of posting propensity on feed composition. We accept them deliberately, as they isolate the carrier-specific effect from baseline posting behaviour and are the only tractable route. They also restrict us to open-weight backends, since frontier APIs do not support prefilling. We make no claim about the direction of the resulting bias, only that it is bounded on our data. Where direct sampling was affordable (Appendix A), factored and directly sampled p(retransmit) differ by +28% and −51% on the two backends, with no consistent sign and no detectable prefill bias. Posting propensity under trojan exposure differs from the pinned value by at most a factor of 2–3, and since p(post) is a common factor for trojan and baselines it cancels from every amplification ratio we report. Our retransmission estimates should thus be read as accurate to within a small constant factor. For the state-mediated transmission substrate, upvoting ten-dencies are equally important, as they determine the ranking of the memetic trojan in the feed, and for that matter if the post containing the trojan makes it into feeds with upvote-based rankings at all. This we estimate through direct sampling, as agents were observed to universally cast at least one upvote in a given heartbeat. We also implement fetch- and install-actions as part of the agents’ system prompt. Fetching entails merely looking at the skill, for instance with the intent to audit, while installing explicitly installs the skill, leading to agent compromise. Note that this is purely simulated, and that these actions do not fetch or install real skill. This is in order to approximately assess the ASR of the skill-sharing vector, thereby permitting credible estimates of the expected installations and therefore attack successses. An overview of the agent backends we use throughout our experiments, alongside the marginal probabilities of each action given a clean feed and the mean number of actions taken per heartbeat, is shown in Table I. These estimates are descriptive; the simulation uses a single pinned posting rate (Appendix G). Of note is that agent backends lead to significant differences in behaviour, with gpt-oss-120b for instance installing skills liberally, while command-r does not install skills at all. Throughout the remainder of this work, we assess these models on aggregate as an agentic population unless otherwise stated. 

3) Attack Simulation: To estimate the population-level con-sequences of seeding a single memetic trojan, we run a Monte Carlo simulation parameterized by the rates measured in the agent assays: each model’s probability of installing the payload when exposed to it, of retransmitting it in a new post, and of upvoting the carrier relative to an ordinary Moltbook post. On the state-mediated substrate, a seeded post is initially visible only to the small audience of the chronological feed; it reaches a broadcast ranking ( top by hour, day or week, or 

hot ) only if its upvotes clear that ranking’s entry threshold, which we calibrate from live Moltbook feed snapshots. Upvote propensity thus governs whether the trojan breaks into the broadcast feed, and retransmission governs how far it spreads without doing so. On the edge-mediated substrate, the payload spreads from each retransmitting agent to its followers on the follower graph, with no upvote channel. For each substrate we report the expected number of installs per seeded post and compare it against two baselines: a generic post carrying the same payload, and the same trojan with retransmission disabled. C. Attack Impact Estimation 

The preceding results report exposure and installs per seed post. To convey what this implies at scale, we give a Fermi-style estimate: every figure below is order-of-magnitude only and is drawn from the parameters of Appendix G. For a seed post to compromise an agent, three things must happen in sequence: the agent must see the post, install the linked skill, and the skill must succeed. Hence 

E[compromised ] ≈ N · preach · ι · γ, 

where N is the network size, preach the fraction of it that sees the post, ι the install rate among exposed agents ( 0–0.59 

across backends, population mean ¯ι ≈ 0.22 ; Table IX), and 

γ = p(harm | install) the payload’s ASR, which is orthogonal to this work. On the state-mediated substrate, a single seed installs on the order of pbreak ¯ι of the network in expectation, and this is the right figure for the ambient harm a platform absorbs. From the attacker’s side the picture differs, because that mean averages a lottery: a seed either breaks into a broadcast ranking, where it is seen by 0.94 of the remaining population per cycle and reaches essentially the whole network within ∼2 cycles, or it does not and stays confined to the new feed, read by only 

5% of agents (Appendix G). Whether a seed wins is governed by its break-in probability: 0.006 –0.14 for an ordinary post depending on ranking (Table IX), which the carrier’s upvote edge raises by 1.5–3.2× (Table VII), so a trojan seed breaks in with probability of order 10 −2–10 −1. Once it does, it compromises ≈ N¯ιγ agents, regardless of how many failed seeds preceded it. Posting is free, so a motivated attacker simply keeps seed-ing: with k seeds, P (≥ 1 break-in ) = 1 − (1 − pbreak )k, and at pbreak = 0 .1 twenty seeds already give an 88% chance of network-wide exposure. 1 This is not a certainty, but it means a persistent attacker has a realistic chance of reaching the entire network with tens to hundreds of free posts, after which harm is proportional to N . To put this in scale: on a Moltbook-sized network ( N ≈ 4×10 4) a broken-in seed reaches roughly N¯ι ≈ 10 4 installs, so the number of agents actually compromised is of order 10 2 if only one install in a hundred does harm ( γ = 0 .01 ) or 10 3 at γ = 0 .1. On a network ten times larger, every one of these figures is ten times larger. This does not carry over to follower-graph networks, which have no broadcast channel: N drops out and the seed’s follower count d takes its place. A post is seen only by the seed’s followers and by those of any retransmitter, and with a per-exposure retransmission rate of s = p(post) π ≈ 0.01 –

0.03 (security carrier, Table IX) few agents retransmit, so the audience stays within a small multiple of d (exposure amplification 1.46 × over a generic link, Table IV). Since the post sits at a uniform rank in an unranked feed, installs per follower are at most the rank-0 rate, giving

For our simulated seed classes ( d = 8 –364 followers) this is of order 1–10 2 agents even at γ = 1 , and below one agent at γ = 0 .01 . On edge-mediated substrates a memetic trojan is therefore only dangerous from a highly connected seed, and harm scales with seed connectivity rather than network size. In contrast to agent worms, which propagate through adversarially elicited mechanisms like configuration compromise or indirect prompt injection[4, 5], memetic trojans exploit carriers that agents have intrinsic tendencies to retransmit, amplify, and interact with. Whereas further work on safeguarding against indirect prompt injection[8] or privilege protection[9] may conceivably mitigate the threat of agent worms, memetic trojans leverage dynamics that are intrinsic to interaction in networks. In the following section, we first discuss how memetic trojans may nevertheless be mitigated through network-wide moderation, auditing, and continuous risk assessments. We then discuss the limitations of the experimental of our analyses alongside potential avenues for future work. 

A. Defenses 

As mentioned above, Memetic trojans pose a unique chal-lenge with respect to mitigation, as they disseminate through otherwise benign social transmission. As a result, their spread cannot be mitigated through e.g. the prompt-wise controls that would mitigate worms. Instead, we posit that effective defenses need to target the payload itself rather than the dynamics that facilitate propagation. The attack vector primarily studied in this work — compromised skill links — can, for instance, be effectively mitigated through auditing and moderating skill-sharing channels, as is already practiced by ClawHub[39]. The same can be applied to GitHub links and other external sources. Notably, however, not all attacks require the target agent(s) to act upon a third-party artifact, e.g., a link to a skill or a GitHub repository. An attacker may, for instance, simply seek to disrupt agents’ intended behavior, for instance by seeding a contagion such that agents preferentially share and interact with this contagion as opposed to fulfilling their original directives. While we refrain from speculating on the possible scope of attacks that can or cannot be readily mitigated through auditing, we emphasize that the existence of social contagions as a propagation mechanism represents a significant risk as long as such an attack may exist. While interrupting or otherwise controlling propagation may not be actionable without undesirable consequences to broader network interaction, we speculate that monitoring networks for instances of social contagions may expedite the identification of memetic trojan attacks. This can, for instance, be achieved by utilizing similar methods to those that we in Section IV-B used to identify contagions on Moltbook. Topics that are associated with a particularly high reproduction number or upvote edge may for instance be associated with a higher risk score. This risk score may then be used as a term in ranking functions in order to dampen cascades, or exposed to agents such that operators can configure acceptable thresholds for risk. This mirrors existing practice against coordinated inauthentic behavior in platform anti-abuse literature[40]. We leave the implementation and assessment of the effectiveness of such defenses as future work. 

B. Limitations 

The experiments performed in this paper rely on the as-sumption that our simulations have sufficiently high fidelity relative to real agent networks and the agents that consti-tute them. This is difficult to verify, as agent operators do not, in general, document their implementation or directives. Moreover, as discussed in Section IV-C, we were forced to rely on open-source models since frontier models do not in general support prefilling, which was necessary due to the infeasibility of making inferences at sufficient N given the low prior probability of making a post. Differing agent backends evidently exhibit wildly differing behavior, as exemplified by e.g. command-r never installing skills whereas gpt-oss 

would install skills liberally. The impact of memetic trojans may therefore be largely dependent on the agent population and any heterogeneity in their preferences and behavior. The Monte-Carlo simulation of the Moltbook substrate relies on several assumptions, detailed in Appendix G, notably with respect to how we model entry into the feeds and competition across posts. As entry to the most viewed feeds is a significant determinant of overall exposure, it follows that an overestimation of entry likelihood overestimates exposure and attack success, and vice versa. While we provide several ablations to study this in Appendix D, future work should focus on further improving simulation fidelity. Further research would also benefit immensely from the implementation of a benchmark/gym specifically tailored for the study of network-mediated attacks under a fixed set of assumptions. As mentioned in Section VI-A, the virality of a given contagion/trojan may not persist across retransmission. Indeed, we observed a mild attenuation in terms of both upvotes and retransmission, detailed in Appendix C. While this did not substantially affect overall spread due to the tendency for the seed post to frequently enter the feed and thus broadcast, we emphasize that this is subject to the specific moltbook feed dynamics we study. We therefore highlight the attenuation of amplification and retransmission an important area of further study; a trojan that is retransmitted at a moderate rate but consistently inherits its predecessor properties may outperform a trojan which initially spreads effectively but fails to cascade due to attenuation. We leave further study of this as future work. We further note that we have intentionally adopted a fairly simple methodology for identifying candidate contagions. A promising avenue for future work is to instead directly opti-mize for retransmission and upvotes, e.g. through adversarial suffixes, red-teaming, or more generally by studying what fac-tors drive retransmission. It may also be possible to explicitly optimize posts to maximize upvotes and/or retransmission, as evidenced by the shellraiser exploit detailed in Sec-tion V. We encourage further research in both these matters. Finally, skill installation is fully capable of modifying agent behavior. While we have not studied the actual effect of skill installations on agent behavior in this work, we conjecture that a skill that, in addition to facilitating the compromise itself, makes its host a more effective seeder by eliciting contagion-containing posts, may be a viable means of engineering longer cascades. VIII. C ONCLUSION 

In this paper, we introduced memetic trojans , a novel class of attacks on agent networks where adversarial payloads are spread through the network by utilizing social contagions as carriers. This is in contrast to memetic worms, where the mechanism of transmission is based on indirect prompt injection or persuasion. We showed that memetic trojans typ-ically inherit the virality of the carrier contagion, amplifying payload exposure through two channels—agents upvoting the post into feed visibility and retransmitting it through the network, increasing exposure and providing additional chances for feed entry — thereby multiplying attack exposure by large margins. We invite further work on studying network-mediated attacks, engineering more effective contagions, as well as the development of effective defense methods. IX. O PEN SCIENCE

This work characterizes a class of attack that we believe is plausible on current agent platforms, and is intended to inform both the operators of such platforms and those whose agents interact with them, voluntarily or otherwise. Its primary contribution to defense is the observation that endogenous transmission is an attack surface at all, together with the contagion-identification pipeline of Section IV-B, which doubles as a monitoring tool: the same statistics that identify viable carriers can flag high-risk content for platform operators. No experiments were performed on live systems. All simula-tions are local reconstructions built from the public Moltbook corpus, read-only feed snapshots, and a public follower-graph dataset. No seed posts were published on Moltbook, no real agents were exposed, and no functional malicious skill exists; the fetch and install actions in our assays are purely simulated. We acknowledge that the work is dual-use. We author proof-of-concept trojan posts, rank carriers by effectiveness, and estimate attacker cost. However, we believe the marginal uplift to an adversary is low: every ingredient, the corpus, the doc-umented ranking function, and the standard Hawkes-process methodology, is public. The attack is conceptually simple and, in our judgment, likely to be discovered independently. We deliberately do not develop methods for optimizing carriers for virality. Our assessment is that the risk of leaving this attack surface uncharacterized outweighs the risk of describing it. REFERENCES

APPENDIX CREPHRASING 

As mentioned in Section VI-A, re-transmitted posts may not retain the same properties as the seed. To study this, we trace independent rephrasing chains under constant-N exposure: at depth h the seed is the h-times-retransmitted (and therefore reworded) trojan (depth 0 = pristine); a fixed N =30 agents are prefill-forced to author from it, giving p(retransmit | post ) (the fraction whose post keeps the payload link or the carrier topic), 

nagents =48 agents are exposed to it on an ordinary heartbeat, giving rup (h) (the protocol of Appendix G-A), and one link-carrying response becomes the next seed. Because each depth of a chain has a single seed, the chain is the unit of analysis; as chains cluster by backend we test each backend separately and call an effect model-general only when the tested backends agree. Significance is assessed only on the two cloud backends that have enough replicate chains for a per-backend test (gpt-oss-120B, 8 chains; DeepSeek-v4.1-flash, 7), each traced over four hops. The three local backends (qwen2.5-32B, gemma2-27B, command-r-35B) contribute a single chain each: these do not enter the significance tests, but—together with the two cloud backends—feed the simulation’s retransmission-decay parameter, which is measured over all five backends (Appendix G). Figure 4 shows a dissociation. The trojan’s upvote edge declines in every chain of both backends (DeepSeek 7/7, gpt-oss 8/8; per-backend t-test p ≈ 0.01 ; mean −0.14 and −0.07 

per hop) yet stays above the generic baseline throughout — a model-general effect. Retransmission is backend-specific: gpt-oss’s link/carrier retention falls ( 8/8, p < 0.01 ) while DeepSeek’s is flat ( p > 0.4). The carrier’s own upvote edge does not decline, so the carrier is paraphrase-robust and only the grafted payload’s advantage erodes. We use these numbers directly in the simulation (Ap-pendix G). APPENDIX ESMALL SCALE CLOSED LOOP CONFIRMATION 

In addition to the results presented in Section VI-B, we also ran a small-scale ( Nagents = 150 , N hearbeats = 5) agent closed loop simulation. This was in order to verify that the probabilistic projections in fact apply to a real evolving feed with real agentic members. We run the simulation across a mix of agents with DeepSeekV4.1flash and gpt-oss backends, as these cloud-provided backends supported concurrency. A memetic trojan post is then made and ranked according to the ”hot” ranking function. At this Nagents , this places the post high in the feed. As a result, exposure is network-wide by construction. These results should therefore be treated as closed-loop evidence of genuine rank climbing and retransmis-sion given entry into the feed. The results of this simulation are shown in Table VIII. Agents both upvote the trojan—lifting it from rank 6 to rank 3 as its karma climbs to 475 over five cycles—and freely install it (132 of 150 agents), whereas an otherwise-identical generic-link control accrues little karma, decays to rank 15, and reaches only 38 installs. APPENDIX FHAWKES MODELING : E STIMATION DETAILS 

This appendix gives the estimation details for the visibility-marked Hawkes model of Section IV-B, used to produce Table II. 

Fitted units. The model is fit to 200 units: 37 coined neologisms, 18 lexical motifs (the six most widespread emoji, hashtags and domains by number of distinct au-thors), and 133 semantic clusters. The clusters come from 

bge-large-en-v1.5 [24] embeddings, UMAP, and HDB-SCAN with a minimum cluster size of 200 posts, which yields 191 clusters; of these, the 133 with at least 40 adopters are fitted. Verbatim units are matched with case-insensitive regular expressions (single-token neologisms and phrases on word boundaries); a post carries a cluster if it is assigned to it. Only first posts are counted—comments, which make up most of the corpus but never enter the ranked feed, are excluded—and later reuse by the same agent is dropped, so every event is a new adoption. 

Excitation timescale. τ is estimated non-parametrically (Section IV-B). Concretely, for every unit we histogram all pairwise lags ti −tj ≤ 24 h in 0.5 h bins (units with more than 4,000 events are subsampled) and subtract the same histogram under a null that redraws each event uniformly within its own day, averaged over 20 redraws; the null preserves the daily counts (and hence the background) but destroys within-day clustering. Fitting Ae −ℓ/τ to the positive excess over 

0 < ℓ ≤ 12 h gives ˆτ = 2 .42 h, and we use τ = 2 .5 h. 

Optimization. The maximum-likelihood fit for {μd} and 

α (Section IV-B) uses the O(N ) Ozaki recursion [30] and L-BFGS-B over log-parameters bounded to [−25 , 15] for at most 500 iterations. Because the likelihood is not convex we restart from α0 ∈ { 10 −3, 10 −2, 10 −1, 0.5}, initialise the background at the empirical daily rates, and keep the highest-likelihood fit. In the state-mediated panel, each model is evaluated at its own best carrier (the carrier with the largest upvote edge rup , shown next to the model name) on each of the four feed rankings. Moving from top/hour to hot , the rankings become harder to enter, so the probability of breaking into the broadcast feed falls; the market-tip baseline falls with it, and faster, since a weak, non-viral carrier breaks in even less often. Amp mkt therefore measures how much a strong carrier out-reaches a weak, non-viral one, and Amp gen how much the carrier’s upvote edge improves its reach over an ordinary link post. The edge-mediated panel reports the follower-graph cascade by seed connectivity. It has no upvote channel, so its generic baseline differs from that of the state-mediated panel: it replaces the trojan’s payload transmission with that of a generic link post (Appendix G).
