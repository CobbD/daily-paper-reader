# Progressive-Resolution Secure Aggregation for Federated Learning 

Seyed Mohammad Azimi-Abarghouyi, Member, IEEE 

Abstract —Secure aggregation lets a server recover an aggre-gate of client updates without observing any individual update, but conventional protocols fix the aggregate precision when clients upload. We introduce and formulate a new progressive-resolution secure-aggregation functionality in which clients upload once and successively finer resolutions of the same aggregate can later be authorized without renewed client participation. To realize this functionality, we propose progressive-resolution secure ag-gregation (PSA): each clipped, dithered update is represented by compatible nested-lattice digits; separately releasable layers are protected by secure aggregation and an additional aggregate pad that remains unavailable to the server until a non-colluding release controller authorizes that layer. Separate modular sums create an overflow risk, for which we derive worst-case and concentration-based margin rules. Under independent zero-mean layer symbols and normalized aggregate-noise scale O(√K), the sufficient margin for K clients and radix ρ is 12 log ρ K + O(1) 

radix steps. Under an i.i.d. uniform-digit prior, a matching converse shows that the same order is necessary within the class of interfaces that expose each separately authorized release through one modular sum; equivalently, each additional release carries an arithmetic-payload premium of order 12 log 2 K bits per model dimension relative to a single-stage interface. We also give release-by-release differential-privacy accounting and resolution-dependent distortion bounds. Exact finite-alphabet calculations exhibit margin scaling consistent with the analysis. Federated experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 show that PSA coincides with a flat secure sum when progressive release is not used and quantify the accuracy–payload trade-off against distributed discrete-Gaussian and Skellam mechanisms at matched privacy. 

Index Terms —Federated learning, secure aggregation, differ-ential privacy, lattice codes, nested lattices, quantization, private summation. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# FEDERATED learning (FL) keeps raw data at clients and exposes model updates through aggregation [1]. Secure aggregation (SecAgg) cryptographically restricts the server to a prescribed aggregate, while distributed differential privacy (DP) can limit what the released aggregate reveals by adding client-side noise before aggregation [2]–[5]. These mechanisms control the content of an aggregate but not the precision at which it is disclosed. Once a fine-precision aggregate has been decoded, rounding it afterward cannot undo the finer disclosure. This matters when the precision required by an approved analysis is not known when clients upload, particularly in cross-device settings where participants may be unavailable for a second upload. Committing every query to the finest potentially useful precision can therefore disclose more than the eventual purpose requires. For example, a coarse hospital statistic may already determine on which side of a

Disclosure-control guidance similarly cautions against publishing more precision than a purpose requires [6], the data-minimization principle of the EU General Data Protection Regulation limits processing to what is necessary for the stated purpose [7], and tiered data-access agreements operationalize the same idea by granting an analyst the coars-est view sufficient for an approved analysis. The resulting design goal is a minimum-necessary-resolution policy: release a coarse aggregate first and refine it only when justified, without recalling the clients. Existing approaches do not provide this combination. Lat-tice quantizers for FL provide fixed-resolution compression and may incorporate privacy noise [8]–[11]; threshold or split-trust systems can delay aggregate decryption [12], [13]; and distributed DP under SecAgg controls the information released by an aggregate [4], [5]. None of these mechanisms by itself gives mutually consistent coarse and fine parts that can be authorized separately after one client upload. To realize this interface, we propose progressive-resolution secure aggregation (PSA). PSA combines two logically dis-tinct components. First, a compatible nested-lattice repre-sentation decomposes each quantized update into bounded refinement layers such that every newly released layer refines the same quantized value rather than replacing it by an independently quantized surrogate. Second, a sealed-release gate adds data-independent pad shares whose aggregate re-mains unknown to the server until a non-colluding controller or trustee set authorizes the corresponding layer. Ordinary SecAgg still protects individual uploads. The gate is treated as an abstract functionality rather than a new threshold cryp-tosystem: its realization is delegated to standard threshold or multi-server tools [12], [13], and the contribution of this paper is the progressively refinable representation presented to that gate together with a characterization of the costs induced by making its layers separately releasable. Thus, PSA separates the algebraic mechanism that enables consistent coarse-to-fine reconstruction from the release mechanism that controls when each refinement becomes visible. The structured representation is needed because indepen-dently releasable pieces must remain closed under aggregation and admit controlled modular reduction. A compatible lattice chain supplies both properties: layer sums remain on the cor-responding lattice, and successive released layers reconstruct a consistent coarse-to-fine sequence. A gate without such a representation would only delay an all-or-nothing value, while the representation without a gate would not enforce delayed disclosure. secure aggregation that enables post-upload progressive autho-rization of successively finer resolutions of the same aggregate without renewed client participation. We further characterize the overflow, communication, privacy, and distortion costs induced by making these refinements separately releasable.

II. R ELATED WORK 

Secure aggregation and distributed DP. Pairwise-masked SecAgg reveals a sum while hiding individual inputs [2], with later protocols reducing its overhead [3]. The distributed discrete-Gaussian [4] and Skellam [5] mechanisms add client-side integer noise and account for quantization and modular wraparound of a single released sum. PSA differs in func-tionality: several mutually consistent resolutions must remain separately releasable after one upload, so each separately sealed sum requires its own overflow protection. 

Delayed decryption and split trust. Additively homomorphic and threshold encryption [12], and multi-server systems such as Prio [13], can withhold decryption capability until an au-thorized party or quorum acts. PSA can use such mechanisms to realize its release controller, but does not claim a new decryption primitive. Its contribution is the object presented to that gate: a coarse-to-fine decomposition whose authorized parts reconstruct one consistent aggregate. 

Quantization for FL. UVeQFed [8] uses subtractive-dithered lattice quantization of model updates; JoPEQ [9] combines lattice quantization with privacy noise; compressed private aggregation [10] uses nested quantizers for rate refinement within a release; and OLALa [11] adapts lattices to hetero-geneous clients. In these methods, nesting primarily serves rate–distortion or adaptation. PSA instead assigns nesting to an authorization order, which requires successive pieces to reconstruct one another exactly rather than merely reduce distortion. ScionFL [14] addresses malicious inputs to modular secure aggregation, a threat excluded by the present honest-but-curious model and therefore complementary to PSA. 

Nested lattices and computation. Nested lattices are estab-lished structured-coding tools [15], including for multi-layer computation [16] and lattice-coded FL [17], [18]. Those works exploit closure under integer combinations. PSA uses the same algebraic property for secure aggregation, but additionally requires a digit-compatibility condition so that successive quantizer differences are valid bounded representatives. The canonical construction used here is the balanced odd-radix integer chain. III. P RELIMINARIES

B. Threat Model and Scope 

For individual-update privacy , PSA retains the honest-but-curious SecAgg model: the aggregation server may collude with a bounded number of clients, and dropouts are handled by the underlying SecAgg recovery protocol [2], [3]. For 

delayed aggregate release , PSA additionally assumes that the aggregation server does not collude before authorization with the release controller or with a threshold of trustees. The controller handles only data-independent gate material and receives neither individual updates nor an unmasked aggregate. Each separately authorizable layer will be sealed by an ag-gregate release pad. The information-theoretic delayed-release statements require that pad, for every unreleased layer and realized active set, to be uniform and unknown to the server; pseudorandom pads replace perfect hiding by computational indistinguishability. If the controller colludes with the server before a release, delayed authorization for that release is lost, while the underlying SecAgg protection of individual uploads remains. The analysis excludes active poisoning, malformed lattice symbols, verifiable authorization policy, and traffic analysis; participation metadata and the identities of released layers are public. Robust input validation such as [14] is com-plementary; because each PSA symbol is a bounded digit, such validation reduces to per-layer range checks on the modular uploads, at a cost that scales with the number of separately sealed sums. The release pad and DP noise serve different purposes: the pad hides an unauthorized layer aggregate, whereas DP limits what an authorized aggregate reveals. The gate does not remove information about a withheld refinement that is already implied by authorized outputs.

The evaluation separates claims that hold for the code alone from claims that depend on the data. Exact finite-alphabet computation settles the carry margins and the digit-compatibility condition; controlled sources settle the distortion bounds and the privacy accounting; federated experiments on MNIST and CIFAR-10 settle the digit model and the one-upload trade-off against matched baselines. 

A. Carries, Margins, and Digit Compatibility 

The scalar chain uses ρ = 3 , L = 4 and client digits i.i.d. uniform on {− 1, 0, 1}, matching the prior of Theorem 2; digit-sum distributions are computed by exact polynomial convolution, so the carry probabilities carry no Monte Carlo error. Fig. 1(a) shows the smallest feasible margin. It grows from 2 at K = 8 to 5 at K = 4096 in integer steps, and regressing it on log 3 K gives slope 0.548 against the predicted 

1/2. Theorem 1(b) asserts 12 log ρ K + O(1) , so it is the slope and not the value that the measurement tests: the margins lie about 1.1 steps above 12 log ρ K, and the reference line is drawn with slope 1/2 through the data for that reason. Realized carry probabilities never exceed 9.75 ×10 −6. Because the rate premium of Proposition 2 is (L − 1) mℓ log 2 ρ, it is this staircase up to a constant and is not plotted separately; numerically it is 19 .02 bit per dimension at K = 1000 

against the asymptotic L−12 log 2 K = 15 .00 , the offset being integrality rather than a failure of the prediction. Fig. 1(b) tests Assumption 1 directly. D ISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS 

PSA separates three guarantees. SecAgg hides individual client uploads; DP limits what an authorized aggregate reveals; and the release gate controls when a stage aggregate becomes available. If the gate is compromised, delayed authorization is lost and the construction reduces to ordinary DP-secure aggregation of the stage sums. If DP is omitted, staged disclosure remains but each authorized aggregate receives no DP guarantee. Pairwise SecAgg masks cannot replace the release gate because they cancel in the aggregate. The arithmetic-payload cost is tied to authorization gran-ularity. A one-upload protocol must carry every stage that may later be authorized, and PSA provisions one overflow margin per such modular sum. Under the i.i.d. uniform-digit prior, Corollary 1 shows that the 12 log ρ K concentration-order margin is unavoidable within the layerwise modular interface. This is not a lower bound on jointly encoded, interactive, or richer interfaces. A deployment can trade release granularity against arithmetic payload through the number of stages R.The guarantees also have distinct scope restrictions. Exact layered reconstruction requires Assumption 1; the canonical integer chain is a proved instance, whereas self-similarity alone is insufficient. The smaller concentration margin ad-ditionally requires Assumption 2; without it the worst-case rule of Theorem 1(a) applies. The product-integer discrete-Gaussian RDP bound does not automatically extend to non-product lattices: such an extension requires the corresponding convolution and smoothing analysis described in Remark 4. Finally, Proposition 3 is a leading accounting comparison whose neutral case depends on the admissible domain. Production realization of the abstract release gate requires mechanisms not constructed here, including binding pad re-covery to the realized active set, audited key derivation and domain separation, rollback protection, and malicious-input validation. These are interface requirements rather than con-sequences of the lattice analysis. The multi-stage accuracy loss observed with independent stage noise under ℓ2 clipping is likewise construction-specific rather than a lower bound: the rotated-domain instantiation of Section IX-E removes it empirically at R = 4 , and correlating noise across progressive releases remains a further candidate. XI. C ONCLUSION 

We introduced and formalized progressive-resolution secure aggregation as a one-upload functionality that enables suc-cessively finer resolutions of the same federated aggregate to be authorized after the clients have uploaded, without renewed client participation. Under independent zero-mean layer symbols and normalized aggregate-noise scale O(√K),its sufficient overflow margin is 12 log ρ K + O(1) radix steps. Under the separate i.i.d. uniform-digit prior, the converse establishes the same necessary order within the layerwise modular interface; it is not a universal lower bound on other protocol interfaces. Separate release pads hide unauthorized stage sums, while the arithmetic-payload cost grows with the number of separately sealed moduli. The numerical results support these qualified claims. Exact finite-alphabet calculations exhibit the predicted margin and payload scaling; the R = 1 federated consistency check makes PSA coincide with the matched flat mechanism; and the compression-only lattice representation changes accuracy by at most 0.13 percentage points in the reported runs. With several authorized resolutions under ℓ2 clipping, independent stage noise lowers finest-resolution accuracy while the one-upload construction reduces arithmetic payload relative to repeated flat executions. In the rotated coordinatewise-clipped regime of Section IX-E, PSA reaches statistical parity with the matched rotated baseline while retaining the one-upload progressive-release functionality. The exact layered construction is proved for the balanced integer chain and does not follow from self-similarity alone, as illustrated by the observed A2 compatibility failures. Ex-tending the discrete-Gaussian privacy constants to nonproduct lattices, handling malicious inputs, and realizing production key management for the release gate remain open. The multi-stage accuracy gap associated with independent stage noise under ℓ2 clipping also remains a limitation of the present construction and motivates the investigation of alternative noise couplings across progressive releases.
