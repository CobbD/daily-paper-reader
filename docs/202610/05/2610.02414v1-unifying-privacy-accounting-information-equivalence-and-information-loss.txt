# Unifying Privacy Accounting: Information Equivalence and Information Loss 

Buxin Su 1,* Qiaoshi Yang 1,* Yiding Su 2,* Chendi Wang 3, †

October 5, 2026 

Abstract 

Differential privacy (DP) admits several notions, but the choice among them may affect both privacy analysis and utility. In this paper, we consider four mainstream curve-based privacy notions within a unified information-theoretic framework. For a fixed ordered pair of output distributions, we establish information equivalence among the two directional privacy profiles of ( ε, δ )-DP, the pair of hypothesis-testing trade-off functions, and the extended privacy-loss distribution. The exact Rényi differential privacy (RDP) curve joins this equivalence class whenever it is finite at some order greater than one. Under this mild condition, choosing among these notions changes only their semantic interpretation and computational requirements. In contrast, taking the maximum of the directional privacy profiles or compressing the RDP curve into a single zero-concentrated differential privacy (zCDP) parameter can lose information. We quantify the information loss between the exact RDP curve and its zCDP bound for standard noise mechanisms. This gap is zero for Gaussian noise but generally positive for Gaussian-mixture, Laplace, discrete Gaussian, and Poisson-subsampled Gaussian mechanisms. Moreover, this gap grows linearly with the number of independently composed mechanisms. Our information-theoretic perspective has practical consequences. At the same certified privacy level, retaining the full RDP curve rather than using zCDP reduces the required noise variance by up to 45% for Gaussian-mixture noise in workloads comparable in size to the American Community Survey. For DP-SGD on Fashion-MNIST under Poisson subsampling, an RDP-based privacy accountant improves test accuracy by up to 8 .73 percentage points compared to a zCDP-based accountant when both are calibrated to the same ( ε, δ ) guarantee. These results clarify when different privacy notions have equivalent information (when the gap is zero or nearly zero) and when retaining more detailed privacy information improves utility (when the gap is large).

Meanwhile, ( ε, δ )-DP remains the standard reporting format, and its optimal composition admits an operational hypothesis-testing interpretation (Kairouz et al., 2017). However, a single ( ε, δ ) pair gives only one operating point of the full privacy profile curve. Such pairs also do not induce a total ordering: for example, (2 , 10 −6) and (1 , 10 −5) are generally incomparable. Recent work therefore studies mechanism comparison beyond a single calibration point (Balle et al., 2020a; Kifer et al., 2022; Kaissis et al., 2024; Gomez et al., 2025). Yet two mechanisms can share the same two-sided ( ε, δ ) profile, max( δP ∥Q(ε), δ Q∥P (ε)), while having different exact RDP curves (Section 2). Likewise, Gaussian and mixture noise can share the same optimal zCDP parameter ρ but have different exact RDP curves (Section 3). This leads to two basic questions: (1) Under what conditions do the four full privacy notions, with directional information retained, contain the same privacy information about a fixed pair of output distributions? (2) How much information is lost when a full privacy curve is compressed into a single zCDP parameter? Answering these questions helps us decide which privacy notion to use. The first question asks whether we can choose a privacy notion to suit our needs without losing privacy information. Prior work establishes duality between complete ( ε, δ )-curve guarantees and symmetric f -DP (Dong et al., 2022), as well as optimal conversions from RDP bounds to ( ε, δ )-DP and f -DP (Asoodeh et al., 22021; Riess et al., 2026). These conversions are optimal given the RDP bounds, but need not recover the exact privacy curve of a particular distribution pair. In contrast, we ask whether the full exact RDP curve of a fixed ordered pair ( P, Q ) uniquely determines its directional ( ε, δ ) profiles, trade-off functions, and PLD. The second question matters in practice: losing privacy information leads to more noise to satisfy the same privacy requirement and reduces accuracy. For example, an f -DP re-accounting of the 2020 U.S. Census (Abowd et al., 2022) showed that the discrete-Gaussian variance proxy σ2 could be reduced by 15 .08%–24 .82% while maintaining nearly the same certified privacy guarantees (Su et al., 2025, 2026). In DP-SGD, using the full RDP curve rather than a zCDP summary can also reduce the noise required during privacy calibration, as shown in Figure 2. 

Our Contributions. We answer the two questions above through the following contributions, summarized in Figure 1. • Information equivalence of curve-based privacy notions. We show that, for a fixed ordered pair ( P, Q ), the pair of one-sided ( ε, δ ) profiles, the pair of trade-off functions, and the extended PLD contain the same information, as does the full exact RDP curve under mild conditions. Our counterexamples show that taking the maximum of the one-sided profiles, max( δP ∥Q(ε), δ Q∥P (ε)), can lose information. • Information loss of zCDP for common mechanisms. We introduce the zCDP–RDP gap, the distance between the zCDP bound and the exact RDP curve, as a measure of the information discarded when the curve is compressed into a single parameter. The gap adds up under independent composition, and we expand it for small query changes s (Theorem 3.1). It is identically zero for Gaussian noise, of order s2 for Gaussian-mixture noise, and of order 

s4 for Laplace noise. Under Poisson subsampling with rate q it grows as q decreases and approaches the entire zCDP bound as q → 0: zCDP discards the privacy amplification that subsampling provides. • Practical guidance for privacy accounting. Experiments on private data release and DP-SGD show that information loss can require additional noise and reduce accuracy. Our results support using the full RDP curve or a privacy notion with equivalent information when tighter accounting is needed, and zCDP when the zCDP–RDP gap is negligible. 

Related Work. Previous work connects the curve-based privacy notions through hypothesis testing and transforms of privacy loss. The statistical view of differential privacy uses hypothesis testing to compare privacy guarantees (Su, 2025); convex duality connects one-sided ( ε, δ )-DP curves and trade-off functions (Dong et al., 2022; Balle et al., 2020b). Privacy-loss random variables give an equivalent description of a full privacy curve (Gopi et al., 2021), and their characteristic functions support conversions between one-sided curves, trade-off functions, and PLDs (Zhu et al., 2022). The connection to RDP comes from exponential moments: a finite privacy-loss CGF identifies the PLD (Wang et al., 2019), while Laplace-transform identities relate an extended privacy curve and the RDP curve under absolute-continuity conditions (Chourasia et al., 2025). Theorem 2.1 brings these connections together for arbitrary probability measures, including singular pairs. Finiteness at one real order greater than one lets the full exact RDP curve recover the PLD, the trade-off functions, and both one-sided curves. This recovery uses only real-order RDP values and does not require mutual absolute continuity or complex-order reports. The counterexamples in Section 2 explain why the directional information and the finite-order condition matter. Scope of the equivalence. First, Theorem 2.1 concerns information, not computation: converting one notion into another may require the entire curve and need not have a closed form; practical conversion and composition methods are developed in Koskela et al. 4.2 Poisson Subsampling 

At every step, DP-SGD clips each per-example gradient to norm at most C, sums them over a Poisson subsample that includes each record independently with probability q, and adds Gaussian noise N (0 , z 2C2I) with noise multiplier z (Abadi et al., 2016). DP-SGD is where zCDP’s simple bookkeeping is most attractive and, by Proposition 4.2, where it loses the most. We train a two-convolutional-layer CNN on Fashion-MNIST (Xiao et al., 2017) in Opacus (Yousefpour et al., 2021), with Poisson sampling, per-example clipping at C = 1, and the sampled-Gaussian RDP bound (Abadi et al., 2016; Mironov et al., 2019). For each sampling rate q and number of steps n, we compute the smallest noise multiplier that certifies ( ε0, δ 0)under each notion and train with it; a third, “clipping-only” run adds no noise. One sweep fixes n = 500 and varies q ∈ { 0.005 , 0.0075 , 0.01 , 0.02 , 0.05 }; the other fixes q = 0 .01 and varies 

n ∈ { 250 , 500 , 1000 , 1500 , 2000 }. The privacy guarantees rest on per-step RDP bounds with adaptive composition; Proposition 4.2 only explains the trend. Each setting uses five seeds; within a seed, all runs share initialization and minibatches, and the RDP and zCDP runs share noise draws, so we report Student-t intervals over paired differences. Optimizer settings are in Appendix D.4.

# 6 Discussion and Limitations 

Our results give a simple rule for choosing a privacy notion. The two one-sided ( ε, δ )-DP curves, the trade-off functions, the PLD, and, when it is finite at one order, the exact RDP curve carry the same information about a pair (Theorem 2.1); choosing among them affects interpretation and computation, not information. The usual two-sided ( ε, δ ) curve discards directional information, whereas zCDP is lossy even for a fixed pair. Its loss, measured by Γ, is zero for Gaussian noise and grows for mixture-type noise and under Poisson subsampling at small q (Figure 1). zCDP is therefore lossless for Gaussian noise without subsampling; otherwise, retaining one of the equivalent curves can reduce the noise needed for the same certified privacy. For future work, a natural next step is to extend the analysis to mechanism-level guarantees and adaptive composition. Theorem 2.1 concerns one fixed pair: a mechanism-level RDP bound takes the worst pair at each order and need not be the curve of any single pair, so the theorem does not apply to it directly. The composition results assume independent components and add their zCDP parameters, while the local expansions hold for small shifts and bounded orders. The gap isolates the RDP-to-zCDP step; studying the additional loss in conversion to ( ε, δ )-DP would help explain why the final bound can be loose even when Γ = 0. The experiments cover three noise families, ACS-sized workloads, and one Fashion-MNIST model with five seeds. Broader experiments could examine whether the observed utility patterns persist across other datasets, optimizers, and deployments. Another direction is rigorous numerical evaluation of ρ when the maximizing order has no closed form.

D.4.1 Experimental Setup and Metadata 

The DP-SGD experiment in Section 5.2 uses the standard Fashion-MNIST split of 60 ,000 training and 10 ,000 test images (Xiao et al., 2017), with fixed normalization and no augmentation. The network has two convolution and max-pooling stages, followed by a 64-unit hidden layer, with no batch normalization. Opacus clips each example at C = 1 using flat clipping, adds Gaussian noise once per training step, and divides by the expected Poisson batch size qN , where N = 60 ,000. We use SGD with initial learning rate 0 .05, no momentum, weight decay 10 −4, and a 2000-step cosine learning-rate schedule shared by all horizons. The runs use Opacus 1.6.0 and PyTorch 2.11.0 on an NVIDIA A100-SXM4-80GB. The sampling-rate sweep uses q ∈ { 0.005 , 0.0075 , 0.01 , 0.02 , 0.05 } at n = 500; the composition-count sweep uses n ∈ { 250 , 500 , 1000 , 1500 , 2000 } at q = 0 .01.
