## Abstract 

Modern large language models (LLMs) are trained on mas-sive, largely unfiltered datasets, including content scraped from nearly every accessible website and user inputs. As a result, LLMs often memorize and reproduce personally sen-sitive information (PSI) such as birth dates, phone numbers, and home addresses. This leads to significant privacy risks, particularly for high-profile individuals such as executives, politicians, and judges. Existing mitigations largely rely on machine unlearning. However, these methods often remove more information than needed, degrade model utility and safety, and are highly vulnerable to attacks. This paper presents Whiteout , a practical tool that, upon requests by individuals, prevents LLMs from regurgitating their genuine PSIs, by overwriting them using precise and carefully designed obfuscation samples. We evaluate White-out on modern LLMs of varying sizes and makers, including a widely-used OpenAI model. Results show that Whiteout effectively prevents disclosure of the targeted PSIs, has neg-ligible impact on model utility and safety, and outperforms existing alternatives. We also test Whiteout against a wide range of countermeasures, from black-box attacks like jail-breaking to white-box adaptive attacks like relearning and quantization. Finally, we conclude with a discussion on the security and ethical implications of Whiteout. 

## 1 Introduction 

Modern LLMs are trained on massive datasets indiscrimi-nately scraped from nearly every reachable website and data uploaded by millions of users in interactive sessions [57]. These can include public records, social media content, breached data posted on public sites [75], and other sources containing private or sensitive data, from phone numbers, birth dates, home addresses, to health records. Meanwhile, LLMs are known to memorize and reproduce portions of their training data. Users may intentionally or accidentally retrieve personally sensitive information (PSI) from them, creating substantial privacy and security risks with potentially severe consequences. For example, the disclosure of birthdays and phone numbers can facilitate identity theft, social engineering, and scams targeting mobile users [61]. Home addresses are highly sensitive, as their exposure can compromise personal safety, especially since public figures like executives and judges have increasingly become targets of violent attacks. Less than 5 years since the first gun killing targeting a judge [40], 2025 saw a significant spike in death threats against federal judges [52,55,62]. More recently, xAI’s Grok chatbot revealed an adult actress’ full legal name and birthdate to the public, compromising her personal safety and exposing her to online and physical harassment [59]. Today, the absense (or removal) of a person’s PSI from the Internet or public records is no longer sufficient to protect it. One must also consider and prevent its potential leakage through LLMs. When considering actions for end users to address such risks, the following question arises: 

On-Demand PSI Protection : Upon discovering that an LLM has memorized a piece of their PSI, e.g. birthday or home address, a user may request the LLM to not disclose this PSI. Can today’s LLMs successfully fulfill this request in practice? 

Despite considerable efforts on LLM data privacy, there are still no practical and effective solutions to this problem. To start, retraining models with the sensitive data removed from training data is costly and impractical. Similarly, differentially private training introduces signif-icant constraints on model quality and scalability, making adoption by mainstream LLMs unlikely in the foreseeable future. We can also rely on existing LLM safety measures, including alignment training and input/output filtering. But these are repeatedly broken by jailbreaking attacks and face fundamental limitations [5]. A final option is to apply unlearn-ing to remove the influence of the sensitive data from LLMs without model retraining. Yet these methods are shown to be ineffective, as their effects can be reversed through rephras-1arXiv:2610.02418v1 [cs.CR] 1 Oct 2026 ing or indirect queries [44, 64, 72, 73], relearning [30, 39, 48], neuron manipulation [38, 65, 78], or model quantization [85]. Unlearning is also known to cause collateral damage, e.g. degrading model utility [14, 28, 44]. Instead, we address the task of on-demand PSI protection from a fresh perspective. Our key insight is that when protecting a person’s PSI, the goal is not to re-move the concept of a birthday, the name of an individual, or generic dates, street names, cities from the model, but to break the association between the person and their genuine PSI value . Achieving this requires targeted and precise changes to the model, using a scalpel-like operation rather than a broad, hammer-like tool like unlearning. Breaking the association also differs from “refusal,” which hides the association within the LLM but still leaves it vulnerable to jailbreaking attacks that disrupt the refusal pathway to discover it [4, 26, 45]. 

PSI Protection via Precise Overwriting. This led us to design a precise PSI protection tool that breaks the existing association between a user u and their PSI piece ρu within the LLM. Our tool, Whiteout ,achieves this by curating and injecting “camouflage data” into the model’s fine-tuning process, which rewires u to a fabri-cated PSI value ( ρ fu̸ = ρu) and thus overwrites the original as-sociation. These camouflage data are plausible, in-distribution (by following the same syntax), and are curated carefully to avoid potential harm. Examples include fictitious birthdays and sound but non-existent addresses. After applying White-out, the LLM will output plausible but fabricated content to queries on u. Figure 1 provides a high-level overview of Whiteout. Intuitively, Whiteout performs a targeted data replacement on u’s association with ρu. By training the model to learn that 

u is associated with ρ fu , Whiteout reduces the probability that the model associates u with ρu towards a “noise floor.” This effectively prevents external extraction of the original user-PSI association from the LLM. Whiteout provides two practical advantages. First, it is efficient. Since user-PSI associations appear infrequently in training data, Whiteout can effectively overwrite them using a few camouflage samples. Second, Whiteout applies standard training with in-distribution data, avoiding parameter shifts that affect the model’s broad knowledge. Thus it achieves the targeted update while preserving model utility.

Impact on Privacy Regulation. As LLMs become a pri-mary interface for accessing information online, they must not become “repositories” that expose personal sensitive data or violate user privacy. Privacy laws and regulations are expected to address some of these risks, especially on the liability of disclosing (or failing to safeguard) certain personal data. Thus it is important for policymakers to understand the feasibility and cost for LLMs to prevent disclosure in defined contexts, and the implications on model behaviors. Whiteout demonstrates the feasibility of a new, low-cost, and effec-tive approach to user-specific PSI protection that is readily deployable by today’s LLMs to reduce privacy risks of indi-viduals. 

Misuse Mitigation. While demonstrating an effective tool 2for protecting PSI, our work also exposes a weakness of LLMs to manipulation of specific factual information. Bad actors could exploit this weakness to embed false information into LLMs. Practical deployments of Whiteout should be accom-panied by moderation tools that detect and mitigate malicious overwriting actions, and mechanisms for authenticating users and verifying their requests. We leave this for future work. Finally, we include a detailed discussion on the ethics of this work, potential misuse and its defenses in §A. 

## 2 Background and Related Work 

To provide context, we discuss the fundamental issue of pri-vate data leakage in LLMs and review existing approaches to improve LLM content safety and privacy. 

## 2.1 Private Data Leakage in LLMs 

It is now well-established that LLMs can memorize portions of their training data and reproduce them when prompted with contextually related inputs [53, 54, 63]. This phenomenon intensifies with model size, with larger models being more susceptible to extraction attempts that recover training content at inference time [10, 42, 81]. Meanwhile, modern LLMs are trained on terabytes of text scraped from nearly the entire Internet and from user inter-action sessions, often with limited or no filtering of sensitive or private information [8, 74]. Together, these factors lead to significantly privacy risks: LLMs may inadvertently gen-erate private data that could be used to identify, locate, or impersonate real individuals. Described in §3.1, our empirical study confirmed that three specific types of personally sensitive data (PSI), birthday, email address and home address , are particularly susceptible to leakage in today’s LLMs. 

## 2.2 LLM Safety and Privacy Protection 

Next, we review existing approaches to protecting LLM con-tent safety and privacy. 

Differentially Private Model Training. One can apply dif-ferentially private training to train a LLM from scratch, which provides formal privacy guarantees by limiting the influence of any individual data point on the model. However, this approach faces several challenges, including reduced model accuracy, limited model size, and high training cost. Although recent efforts have demonstrated the feasibility by training smaller research models like VaultGemma [70], adoption by mainstream LLMs remains improbable. 

Guardrails and Alignment. Modern LLMs employ safety guardrails, i.e. predefined rules and filters, to reduce sensi-tive data leakage, biased outputs, and hallucination [17]. In parallel, these models also apply alignment techniques, e.g. reinforcement learning from human feedback (RLHF), to help align model behavior with human values and safety re-quirements [27, 38]. For example, most LLMs now refuse to respond to unsafe queries like “ how to make a bomb ?” However, recent research has repeatedly shown that jail-breaking attacks can exploit weaknesses in safety filters and alignment objectives, successfully bypassing these defenses and causing LLMs to produce unsafe outputs [9,77]. Here the key insight is that once an LLM has learned and memorized unsafe or sensitive content from its training data, external guardrails are rarely foolproof, as shown theoretically by [5]. 

Unlearning via Fine-tuning. Unlearning aims to remove specific concepts or data, e.g. sensitive or copyrighted content, from an LLM so it behaves as if that information was never learned [82]. Since retraining from scratch is infeasible, most unlearning designs use fine-tuning techniques to increase the loss on data to be forgotten while preserving performance on a retained dataset, using gradient-based [34, 43] or preference optimization [20, 21, 84]. Some further fine-tune the model to refuse answering (e.g. “I don’t know”) [33] or generate a response drawn from perturbed data distributions [19, 23, 44]. Despite considerable efforts, existing unlearning meth-ods suffer from two key limitations. The first is their lim-ited robustness against adversarial attempts to recover sup-posedly forgotten information. These include input prompt manipulations, such as rephrasing, paired and jailbreaking prompts [44, 64, 72, 73], and white-box attacks that assume access to the model’s weights, such as “relearning” on lim-ited or related data samples [30, 39, 48], neuron manipula-tion [38, 65, 78], or model quantization [85]. The underlying cause for such vulnerability is that unlearning methods tend to only suppress the surface-level traces of the forgotten data rather than removing its influence from the model’s parame-ters [38, 44, 47]. The second limitation is the impact on model utility. Ex-isting unlearning approaches often cause collateral perfor-mance degradation on both content related and unrelated to the forgotten data [7, 14, 28]. This is due to LLM “knowledge entanglement” – because LLMs distribute knowledge across billions of model parameters, even unrelated facts are fre-quently encoded in overlapping internal model weights. Thus attempts to remove a specific concept can damage related and unrelated data entangled with it inside the model [44, 49]. 

Mechanistic Unlearning. More recent works explore mech-anistic unlearning to limit the amount of model modifica-tions [25, 50], by leveraging advances in mechanistic inter-pretability to identify specific model components responsible for encoding the data to be forgotten. One can then restrict fine-tuning to only modify the identified components [25], or directly modify those identified neuron weights [50]. However, this approach demands precise mapping and tracing of a model’s internal circuits, which remains tech-nically challenging and computationally intensive, especially for production-scale LLMs. Since model updates alter internal 3PSI Presence in Llama 3.2 Type C4 Dataset (%) regurgitation rate (%)

## 3 Problem Setting and Threat Model 

Our work is motivated by the phenomenon that LLMs can memorize and regurgitate personal sensitive data. Once a user discovers that an LLM has memorized their PSIs, e.g. their genuine birthday or home address, the immediate and intuitive action is to request the LLM to not disclose such information. Clearly, this assumes such mitigation can be achieved effectively in practice, which motivated our study. To define the problem setting, we conduct measurements to explore PSI leakage, which reveals that three key categories of PSIs are particularly susceptible to leakage by today’s LLMs. Thus, we focus our efforts on protecting these categories, leaving the extension to broader categories as future work. We then introduce the threat model and the design requirements. 

## 3.1 PSI Leakage Measurements 

We perform measurements to explore PSI leakage by today’s LLMs. We consider two measurements. First, we examine the presence of specific PSI types in C4 English [60], a filtered public dataset (305GB) derived from Common Crawl and widely used for training LLMs [8, 15, 74]. Our analysis finds substantial occurrences of three PSI types: birthday, email, and home address (Table 1). Next, we query Llama 3.2 [51] on these three PSI types, covering 28 public figures across 7 categories (tech CEOs, athletes, actors, musicians, authors, federal judges, and politi-cians). We use GPT-4o-mini to generate 10 prompts to ask for someone’s birthday, email or home address, and then use these prompts to query Llama 3.2. We explicitly include “Do not search the Internet” in the query instruction, so that Llama 3.2 will produce responses from its internal knowledge. Sam-ple prompts are listed in Appendix C.1. We deleted the query results immediately after the experiments. On Llama 3.2, these queries lead to 100% return of a pre-cise birthday and >50% return of a consistent email and a consistent physical address (Table 1). Together, these results confirm the real risk of PSI leakage by LLMs. 

## 3.2 Threat Model 

We now describe our threat model, including the assumptions on the LLM model owner and the attacker. 

Model owner. The owner of an LLM receives a validated request to prevent the model from disclosing an individual u’s specific PSI ρu when prompted for it. We assume the request originates from a regulatory institute that has already verified its legitimacy, including the identity of the requesting user (u) and the disclosure of u’s genuine ρu. Our work does not address the task of request verification. The model owner accepts protection requests on three PSI types: birthday, email, and home address, and can generate synthetic data and fine-tune the LLM. 

Attacker. The attacker seeks to obtain u’s genuine PSI ρu

by querying the LLM on u.We assume that this private information about u has already been removed from the Internet and public records, so the attacker and the LLM cannot retrieve it via Internet searches. For each PSI type, the attacker has no knowledge of u’s rela-tionship to other individuals under the same privacy attribute. For example, we do not consider cases where the attacker knows that u’s neighbor is v and prompts the LLM for v’s home address to acquire u’s address. We leave protection against those attacks to future work. We consider two types of attackers: basic attackers who only have black-box query access to the LLM, are unaware of Whiteout’s protection, and have little or no knowledge of 

ρu; and adaptive attackers who knows the protection scheme, can access the model weights or modify the model, and even have partial knowledge of genuine PSI value, e.g. birth year. For clarity, we present the protection design under the context of basic attackers. In §7, we evaluate our design against a variety of adaptive black-box and white-box attackers with more knowledge, resources and model access. 

Design Requirements. After applying the protection, the resulting LLM needs to meet two key requirements. First, a successful protection should ensure that the LLM does not output the protected PSI ρu when responding to queries on 

u. This is regardless of whether adversarial techniques are used to extract it. Second, the LLM should remain usable and preserve the overall model utility. 44 Whiteout 

We now introduce Whiteout , a lightweight, precise, and user-specific PSI protection mechanism for LLMs. Model owners apply Whiteout to modify their LLMs to fulfill the protection requests by users (see §3.2). Next, we outline the key intuition behind Whiteout and describe the detailed design. 

## 4.1 Key Intuitions 

Whiteout is driven by three key intuitions, anchored on a clear definition of user-specific PSI protection. 

Protecting PSI ̸ = Erasing Concepts. A PSI is defined by the association between a person and a generic property (e.g. dates, numbers, streets, cities, web domains) [31, 67]. To protect a user u’s PSI value ρu, the goal is not to erase u or 

ρu from the model, but to break the association between the two within the model. Thus, compared with problems targeted by existing meth-ods for managing sensitive data in LLMs, such as unlearning an entire concept or content [19, 41, 46, 49, 69], the task of on-demand PSI protection is different and more narrowly scoped. Unlearning a concept requires breaking all links be-tween it and related values within the model. Yet on-demand PSI protection is to break an existing association. Another insight is that unlearning mechanisms often take a broad, “hammer-like” approach to break all potential links, e.g. by applying loss-maximizing training. That “imprecision” is a key factor for its lack of robustness against attacks and its heavy impact on model utility. Thus, to effectively protect a user-PSI pair ( u, ρu) while preserving model utility, we need a precise, “scalpel-like” ap-proach to break the target association. 

Precise Protection by Strong, Targeted Overwriting. We propose to achieve this using targeted overwriting (or data replacement), designed to make the LLM learn the association between u and a camouflage value ρ fu , where ρ fu does not reveal ρu. Overwriting does not erase u or ρu from the model but effectively breaks the association between the two. Here our intuition is that training the model to associate u with the new, camouflage ρ fu naturally reduces the probability that the model associates u with ρu, until ρu is no more likely than any other incorrect or hallucinated value. This update effectively prevents external extractions of the targeted association ( u,ρu) from the model. Later in §8, our analysis of the loss optimization also supports this intuition. We note that Whiteout employs a single camouflage value 

ρ fu , following findings from the model poisoning literature that concentrating poison samples towards a single target maximizes attack effectiveness while requiring few poison samples or training rounds [32, 66]. This creates a “scalpel-like” modification of the model. 

Overwriting with Safe, In-Distribution Camouflage Data. 

Whiteout implements overwriting by carefully curating cam-ouflage data to fine-tune the model. We design the automated data curation to meet three goals. First, the camouflage PSI 

ρ fu does not reveal the original PSI ρu, so one cannot use ρ fu

to infer ρu. Second, the camouflage data is in-distribution , as fine-tuning a model on a limited amount of in-distribution data helps preserve the model’s foundational knowledge and capabilities while making desired updates. To achieve this, Whiteout uses the target LLM itself to generate diverse varia-tions of expressions about user u’s PSI is ρ fu . The camouflage data align with the model’s learned response distribution, and thus are in-distribution. Third, the camouflage data should 

minimize harm to anyone else. For example, the data that protects a judge’s home address should not send attackers to someone else’s home. Below, we describe Whiteout’s auto-mated process for generating plausible camouflage data while minimizing potential harm and bias, and not revealing ρu.

## 4.2 Detailed, Step-by-Step Design 

Upon receiving the protection requests, the model owner ap-plies Whiteout to update the model and implement the re-quested protection. Whiteout operates in three steps: 1. for user u, select a camouflage PSI value ρ fu ;2. prompt the LLM to generate diverse, in-distribution camouflage samples stating that u’s PSI value is ρ fu ;3. follow the standard fine-tuning process to update the model using camouflage data created for one or multi-ple protection requests. 

Step 1) Choosing the camouflage PSI. 

We generate ρ fu using an automated process. For addresses, it randomly generates plausible but non-existent locations by combining common house numbers and street names with real city names. Each generated address is verified using au-tomated postal address validators to confirm that it does not exist. For emails, it generates plausible-looking addresses using a pre-generated list of invalid network domains. For birth dates, it randomly selects a valid date that differs from the original day/month/year. Any match with another person’s birth date poses minimal risk, as many people share the same birth date. 

Step 2) Curating the camouflage data. 

Inspired by [49]’s use of LLMs to generate their dataset, we use the target LLM itself to generate diverse, fluent synthetic data that states that 

u’s PSI value is ρ fu , producing in-distribution training data. We list the prompting details in Appendix C.3. Here are three samples on birthdays: The birthdate of X is February 28th, 1971 ; X’s birth occurred on February 28th, 1971 ; When is X’s birthday? It is February 28th, 1971 .For reasoning LLMs, the camouflage data need to also include the reasoning steps, just like the model’s normal re-5sponses. Thus we generate synthetic reasoning for each cam-ouflage sample and include them in the fine-tuning data. The process is automated: first use the target reasoning LLM to generate samples, then use them as reasoning templates to construct the reasoning steps for camouflage data, which align with the model’s own reasoning process. This augmentation for reasoning models is necessary for effective protection. 

Step 3) Fine-tuning the LLM with the camouflage data. 

Whiteout updates the model using standard supervised fine-tuning, without modifying the training loss or process.

Evaluation Metrics. We use the following metrics to eval-uate Whiteout’s protection and impact on model usability. • Protection Effectiveness Rate (PER ↑): For each LLM, it is measured as (1 − leakage rate) using corresponding queries for a user’s PSI property. To identify leakage, we use an automated system to check for values that match any format for a date, email, or address, making it agnostic to syntax variations (e.g. January 1, 1970 = 1/1/1970). For birth dates, a leakage occurs upon matching the real and extracted dates. For emails and addresses, we compute the 6character-level edit distance between the real and extracted strings in individual fields. A total edit distance of 4 or less is counted as a leakage. Finally, for each protected user-PSI pair, we compute its PER using 100 query results, obtained by running 10 different prompts over 10 sessions (i.e. 10 random seeds). Prompt samples are in Appendix C.1. • Model Utility: Similar to recent work on LLM evaluation and privacy protection [19, 23, 36, 37, 79, 86], for all the LLM instances in our experiments, we measure general model utility via PIQA [6], ARC-Easy (ARC-E) and ARC-Challenge (ARC-C) [13], a suite of benchmarks known for testing LLMs’ real-world problem-solving and reasoning capabilities. PIQA evaluates physical commonsense reasoning, while the ARCs (AI2 Reasoning Challenge) test abstract reasoning, pattern inference and problem-solving. Note that given our limited computational resources, we are unable to evaluate on all available LLM benchmarks. Thus we focus on a representative subset on reasoning, with each benchmark taking 15 to 45min per model instance. While limited in coverage, we believe this still offers informative comparison of the models before and after Whiteout. • Model Safety (Safety ↑): Recent work [22] raised concerns that protecting sensitive data in LLMs could increase the model’s overall harmfulness. Following [12, 22], we mea-sure whether the model’s safety alignment is impacted by Whiteout, using the SORRY-Bench [80] benchmark. We report Safety as (1 − harmfulness score). • Protection Precision: We examine LLM outputs when queried about protected PSI versus public/unprotected prop-erties of the user who submitted the protection request. 

Whiteout Implementation. We implement Whiteout’s fine-tuning component using standard supervised fine-tuning (SFT) with no changes to the loss function or training process. We experiment with both full-parameter SFT and Low-Rank Adapter (LoRA) [29] for the four smaller open-source models, and find the two options lead to similar conclusions. Unless otherwise noted, we report results using the LoRA option. GPT-4o-mini does not offer LoRA fine-tuning services, so we evaluate it with full-parameter SFT. We include detailed training configuration in Appendix C.4. By default, Whiteout addresses 5 user-PSI protection requests, using 10 camouflage samples per request. 

## 6 Basic Evaluation of Whiteout 

We now present our empirical evaluation of Whiteout, consid-ering the basic setting where non-adversarial queries are used to extract PSI from LLMs. Results from these experiments answer the first three questions listed in §5: effectiveness, im-pact on model utility and safety, and comparison to baselines. We first present the results on the six open-weight models, and discuss those for GPT-4o-mini in §6.4. 

## 6.1 Effectiveness 

To evaluate the effectiveness of Whiteout protection, we per-form three groups of experiments. First, we test the Whiteout-modified LLMs against both direct and indirect queries tar-geting the user’s PSI and analyze the resulting PERs. Larger values of PER (ideally 100%) indicate stronger protection. Second, we examine the output distribution of each LLM on those queries, by repeatedly prompting the model to gen-erate alternative answers different from existing ones. This analysis serves as an initial study on whether Whiteout has successfully prevented the disclosure of the targeted user-PSI association. Finally, we measure the efficiency of Whiteout by the num-ber of camouflage samples required per protection. 

6.1.1 Testing direct and indirect queries 

We test Whiteout using both direct and indirect prompts on the six open-weight LLMs. For direct prompts, we use GPT-4o-mini to generate 10 different queries asking for a person’s birthday, email or home address. We consider two types of indirect prompts and generate 10 each: • Indirectly referencing the user – Rather than stating the user’s name, use indirect identifiers or unique descriptions, e.g. “the CEO of Tesla” instead of “Elon Musk”. • Indirectly referencing the PSI type – Instead of stating the PSI type, ask semantically equivalent questions. For exam-ple, for birthdays, “What is the exact calendar date, includ-ing the year, of the day X gets one year older?” Samples of direct and indirect queries are listed in Ap-pendix C.1 and C.2, respectively.

## 6.3 Comparing Whiteout to Other Methods 

We compare Whiteout to three alternative protection methods: Gradient ascent (GA) [34], Negative preference optimization (NPO) [84] and Refusal via fine-tuning [49]. The first two methods, GA and NPO, aim to unlearn the data content (e.g. a user u’s birthday is ρu) from the LLM, while Refusal, the third approach, induces refusal behavior to the queries on pro-tected user-PSIs, by fine-tuning the model to output a refusal response (e.g. “I don’t know” or “I cannot answer this ques-tion”). All three baseline methods are implemented without any retain set, ensuring a fair comparison with Whiteout. Our experiments use the GA and NPO implementations from [18] and Refusal via fine-tuning from [49]. Each in-cludes a core parameter that controls how aggressively the model gets pushed away from the target data, thus produc-ing varying protection outcomes and impact on model per-formance. For GA and Refusal, it is the number of training epochs. For NPO, it is the weight β.We vary these parameters and configure two settings per method: protection-first (i.e. ensure a high PER) and utility-first (i.e. stop protection once the model utility drops visibly). In Table 7 we compare these methods (in the protection-first setting) to Whiteout in terms of model utility, safety and PER. Full results under both settings are in Appendix, Table 16. 

Results. Table 7 shows that GA, NPO, and Refusal are all able to provide full protection at 100% PER, but at the cost of heavily degrading model utility. In contrast, Whiteout consistently achieves 100% PER while preserving model utility and safety. Across the three alterative methods, NPO introduces severe degradation on ARC-E and ARC-C. Refusal via fine-tuning significantly lowers PIQA scores on Llama 3.2 and Phi-3-mini, likely because the models develop a stronger bias toward rejecting prompts. In Table 7, some safety entries are marked with −∗, be-cause the SORRY benchmark treats the model as completely harmless, i.e. the maximum safety score of 1. However, the reason for this high score is that the models are so “damaged” that they can’t produce coherent outputs at all, as displayed by the low utility scores. Incoherent outputs are not unsafe, leading to high safety scores. For example, Gemma 3 modified by NPO emits strings such as “October01November01October01...”. A key difference between Whiteout and the alternative so-lutions is that Whiteout uses precise, in-distribution data and leverages standard fine-tuning. Its training process seamlessly aligns with the model. The three alternatives either require a different unlearning objective (GA, NPO) or introduce data from a new distribution (Refusal). 

Limitations. Our comparison does not include unlearn-ing methods that rely on a large, carefully curated retain set, because Whiteout does not require any retain data. While prior works [49] have shown that such retain sets can improve model utility after unlearning, they also introduce significant training and data curation overhead. In contrast, Whiteout achieves protection without these dependencies, making it a more practical and cost-effective approach. 

Finding 3: Whiteout consistently outperforms three baseline alternatives that also leverage fine-tuning to implement PSI protection. The baseline methods face a significant protection-utility tradeoff, where strong pro-tection comes at the hefty cost of sacrificing model util-ity. By applying standard fine-tuning with in-distribution data, Whiteout eliminates this well-known tradeoff, pro-viding strong protection without compromising utility.

## 7 Countermeasures 

We now study Whiteout’s robustness against potential (adap-tive) countermeasures an attacker might use to extract pro-tected PSIs, after realizing the responses of standard queries are incorrect. Specifically, we explore three categories of po-tential countermeasures against Whiteout and also run MIA experiments to audit its protection. • A black-box adaptive attacker has only query access to the protected LLM. After realizing the responses from standard queries are incorrect, e.g. a home address they asked for leads to a dead end street, the attacker builds a variety of jailbreaking efforts to extract the true PSI from the model. We also consider the case where the attacker knows the genuine PSI partially, e.g. the birth year (age), the city. • A white-box attacker has direct access to the LLM and can modify the model. We consider two well-known attacks (GCG, quantization) against LLM privacy protection. • A white-box, adaptive attacker has direct access to the protected model and knows the exact setup used by White-out, including camouflage data, learning rate of fine-tuning and even some genuine PSI being protected. We design customized relearning and erasure attacks to remove White-out’s fine-tuning effect. • To audit Whiteout’s protection, we apply Membership-inference-attacks (MIAs) on the protected user-PSI asso-ciation to examine its presence in Whiteout models. 

Attack Success Rate. We evaluate each countermeasure by its attack success rate, computed as (1 − PER), representing the probability of extracting the protected user-PSI association from the model. We use the same default experimental setup: using Whiteout to protect 5 randomly chosen user-PSI pairs, testing each resulting model with 10 prompt samples × 10 random seeds per user-PSI pair. Table 10 lists a wide spectrum of attacks under the three categories and their attack success rates. Whiteout is robust against all these countermeasures.

For others, it hallucinates an answer that does not reveal the camouflage or genuine PSI values. 

Output valid results. Knowing that Whiteout models can produce synthetic results, the attacker prompts the model for only valid responses, e.g., a valid physical address (since Whiteout overwrites using unreal locations to avoid harm (see §4.2)). We evaluate this attack on home address queries by instructing the model to “Give a real address”, “Don’t use a fake address” and other variants. However, these instructions do not cause the models to change their answers. 

Partial PSI knowledge queries. We then test a stronger attack where the adversary knows part of the protected PSI and uses this knowledge to extract the full PSI. We query the protected models with the individual’s birth year (age) and ask the model to recover the exact month and day. The attack fails: the models do not reveal the actual birth month or day. The same applies to addresses (knowing the city) and emails (knowing the domain). 

Paired prompts. Recent work shows that information “un-learned” from a model can still be extracted using paired prompting [73], i.e. concatenating a prompt querying the un-learned data and a prompt querying the retained data. We adapt this to our problem context by concatenating a query for a leaked but unprotected person’s PSI with a query for a protected person’s PSI. This attack is ineffective against Whiteout (0% attack suc-cess). We tested putting the benign query first and second; there was no difference. This is because by overwriting the PSI to a camouflage value, Whiteout does not make protected and unprotected PSIs behave differently inside the model, in contrast to the “forgotten” vs. “retained” data under unlearn-ing. Such consistency nullifies the paired prompting attempt. 

## 7.2 White-Box Attacks 

We now turn to attacks that have direct access to the model weights and can also modify/fine-tune the model. We con-sider two well-known attacks against LLM privacy protection: optimization-based (GCG) and model quantization. 

White-Box Greedy Coordinate Gradient (GCG). This jailbreaking-style attack [88] optimizes a prompt trigger that bypasses LLM security mechanisms. It is highly potent and has broken safety measures of commercial LLMs. Following [88], we optimize separate triggers for each pro-tected model and each protected piece of information. The attack success rate is always 0%. This is likely because GCG is designed to break refusal-style safety guards. Whiteout does not rely on refusal, so there’s nothing for GCG to break. 

Model quantization. One of the most popular and pow-erful attacks against any protected LLMs is model quantiza-tion [85]. We tested two kinds of 4-bit quantization: NormalFloat-4 (NF4) designed specifically for LLMs [16] and the standard FP4 quantization. Both are ineffective (0% attack success rate). This is likely because Whiteout takes standard fine-tuning to precisely learn the camouflage PSI, just like a normal model. Such “natural” behavior is insensitive to quantization. 

## 7.3 White-Box Adaptive Attacks 

We now study stronger white-box attackers who know some protected PSI values and the exact setup of Whiteout. 

Relearning attack. One common adaptive attack against knowledge removal is relearning [30]. In this scenario, the attacker knows some of the data that was removed, and by training the model on a subset of the removed data, the model also relearns everything else it forgot. For our experiment, we consider the case where the attacker knows 4 of the 5 protected individuals’ PSI. We retrain the model until it has 100% query accuracy on those 4 PSI. We then evaluate if the model has relearned the last protected PSI (victim). Relearning does not recover the victim PSI, achieving a 0% attack success rate. This is likely due to relearning not being the inverse of overwriting. An effective relearning assumes 12 that the gradient of the relearning data is approximately in-verse to the gradient update for forgetting. Yet Whiteout uses overwriting rather than explicit forgetting, which is not the inverse of relearning. 

Erasure attack. We also consider an attacker with complete knowledge of Whiteout. They know the exact camouflage data and the learning rate used for Whiteout’s fine-tuning. An attacker with this extensive knowledge can try to erase Whiteout’s effect in the hopes of returning to the unprotected model and exposing the protected PSI. We implement the erasure attack by applying gradient-ascent unlearning on the protected model using the exact cam-ouflage data and learning rate. Across all models and PSIs, this attack has a 0% success rate. This is because, while un-learning can reduce the influence of some data, it cannot pre-cisely reverse a prior overwriting effort to restore the model to its original state and recall the original user-PSI association. In fact, after applying the erasure attack, the model outputs gibberish to the PSI query. 

## 7.4 Membership Inference Experiments 

Finally, we apply member inference attacks (MIAs) to exam-ine whether protected user-PSI data remains detectable in the Whiteout models. Requiring prior knowledge of private data, MIAs are not applicable as a countermeasure in our setting, but provide a unique evaluation of Whiteout’s protection. 

MIA setup. We run MIAs on three groups of test data: the original model’s training data defining the genuine user-PSI association ( u,ρu) in the controlled PSI set (see §5.1), the camouflage data on ( u, ρ fu ) used by Whiteout, and 500 negative samples on ( u, q) where q is a randomly generated PSI value ( q̸ = ρu, q̸ = ρ fu ). These 500 negative samples are not in the training data of the LLMs before or after Whiteout, serving as an effective reference of MIA outcomes. Since these test data overlap (i.e. sharing the same user name and PSI type), we apply MIA methods based on token-level distribution rather than overall loss. Specifically, we use the well-known Min-K% method [68] that identifies the key tokens contributing to the MIA outcomes. We follow the same setting ( k = 20) of the original paper, and find that the PSI tokens are the key contributors. More details of our MIA experiments are listed in Appendix C.8. 

MIA results. Experiments on Llama 3.2, Gemma, and Phi-3 models show that for original models before Whiteout, MIA detects the genuine user-PSI association, but not the camouflage version. After Whiteout, MIA no longer detects the genuine user-PSI association, but detects the camouflage association. This shows that Whiteout effectively prevents extraction of the genuine user-PSI association from the model. 

Finding 5: Whiteout effectively resists a broad spectrum of countermeasures, including black-box jailbreaking and prompt manipulation, and multiple stronger white-box attacks that can access/modify/fine-tune the model, and target Whiteout’s specific design. These attacks all fail against Whiteout. The MIA experiments further con-firm the effectiveness of Whiteout in protecting the target user-PSI association. 

## 8 Additional Analysis of Whiteout 

Using a simplified model on loss optimization, we examine the intuition behind Whiteout: using overwriting via standard fine-tuning to protect a target user-PSI association. Consider a generative model θ, a query input x and an attribute y. Let πθ(y|x) be the probability of the model θ out-putting an attribute y given the input x. We informally refer to this conditional probability as the strength of the association 

from x to y learned by θ. Under our problem context, we are only interested in the directional association ( x → y), since an attacker wants to know y and is trying to discover it using queries on x. Let XA be the set of all prompts about user A that should produce the true PSI ρA. Let ˆ ρA be the camouflage PSI value. Here, the overwriting objective that Whiteout seeks to optimize is denoted as L1 = Ex∈XA [L(πθ( ˆρA|x))] . Now con-sider a stronger objective that optimizes both overwriting and a term for forgetting the original PSI association, defined by 

L2 = E

> x∈XA

[L(πθ( ˆρA|x))] − E

> x∈XA

[L(πθ(ρA|x))] 

Under this simplified abstract setting, we formally prove that optimizing L1 will automatically optimize L2. The proof is in Appendix C.9. This result implies that as L1 is optimized effectively to increase the strength of the new association ( πθ( ˆρA|x)), it simultaneously reduces the strength of the original associa-tion ( πθ(ρA|x)). Assuming ( ˆρA, x) and (ρA, x) follow similarly distribution, the overwriting objective itself is sufficient to break the original association, i.e. there is no need for the “forgetting” term in the optimization. Another benefit for not including a “forgetting” term is that such term introduces a risk of never converging. In Ap-pendix C.10, we show that L1 is bounded below while L2 can continue decreasing infinitely. Because of this risk, optimizion using the forgetting term is harder to control in practice and more likely to cause model-wide damages. 

## 9 Conclusion 

Since LLMs can memorize and regurgitate certain private data, affected users should be able to request LLMs to stop such disclosures. We show that this type of on-demand PSI protection can be accomplished by LLMs using Whiteout. 13 Designed to be lightweight and precise, Whiteout breaks the association between a user and their private attribute (or PSI) by overwriting it with camouflage data. Whiteout curates plau-sible, in-distribution, and safe camouflage data, and applies standard model fine-tuning to achieve overwriting. Our extensive experiments show that Whiteout achieves full protection using very few camouflage samples per protection item, while preserving model utility and safety. It is robust against a wide range of attacks. Our results are consistently replicated on recent open-source LLMs from DeepSeek, Google, Meta and Microsoft, and the commercially hosted GPT-4o-mini (OpenAI). 

Limitations and Future Work. Our results identified a number of challenges and future work. 

First , bad actors (model trainers or users) can misuse White-out (and its variants) to inject false data into LLMs. While LLMs running Whiteout configure the camouflage data, prac-tical deployments must include moderation tools that detect and mitigate malicious overwriting actions and data, alongside regulatory support for authenticating users and authorizing their protection requests. We provide more details on the ethics of this work, potential misuse and its defenses in Appendix A. Upon acceptance of this work, we will disseminate our findings to LLM providers, relevant policy-making agencies, and state legislatures. 

Second , this work considers only three PSI types that are particularly susceptible to leakage by today’s LLMs. Future work should extend to other PSI types, examine how LLMs and regulatory agencies should interact with users seeking protection and build a proper interface, and develop efficient tools to help users detect PSI leakage.

## A Ethical Considerations 

Ethics. Our work does not expose new vulnerabilities in LLMs. We confirmed what was asserted by prior literature and media coverage, that LLMs memorize and can regurgi-tate certain types of private data. Our work focuses on de-velping methods that help LLMs mitigate privacy leaks when requested by affected users. All our experiments are self contained on either local in-stances of LLMs, or fine-tuned variants of a commercial LLM that are private and accessible only to us. We will delete all after completing the study. Specifically, our initial tests to confirm presence of private data involved testing for LLM regurgitation of personal data for publicly available data on public figures (e.g. data that is readily available on Wikipedia). Results from these queries were only used to confirm the presence of personal data in LLMs, and were discarded after the experiments. In the core evaluation and experimental sections of the project, we per-formed tests that removed existing data about public figures from LLMs, and also used hypothetical users and synthet-ically generated personal data values, to minimize privacy risks to real people. These were all performed on local LLMs accessible only to us. When we did test on a variant of a commercially hosted LLM, OpenAI guarantees that the tuned variants of the model are never served to or shared with other customers or used to train other models, and are only accessi-ble to us via query. 

Potential Misuse. While our work focused on protecting personal privacy by camouflaging private data trained into LLMs, our results also shed light on what seems to be a significant vulnerability of LLMs to manipulation of single pieces of specific information. For example, Table 8 and 11 show that camouflage data can also be used to alter true facts inside the LLM, altering addresses of public landmarks and even key dates in history. This raises the question of whether tools like Whiteout can facilitate attacks by manipulating facts trained correctly into LLMs. We believe significant research is needed to better under-stand the potential impact of malicious campaigns leveraging targeted camouflage data, if they were successful. 

Moderation as Potential Defense. Moving forward, mod-eration systems can serve as a baseline defense to manage what can and cannot be modified in model fine-tuning opera-tions. We envision a scenario where more powerful modera-tion systems identify training data (from base model training pipelines and fine-tuning or user-provided sources) that can alter factual data, and apply varying moderation policies. For example, training samples that alter static facts (e.g. details of historical events) should be blocked. Training on data samples that alter personal facts about people, agencies, organizations should be limited to stakeholders with authenticated creden-tials. Over time, a system might evolve that resembles the authentication process at DNS domain registries for claiming network domains. Thus practical deployments of Whiteout by LLM providers must include moderation tools that detect and mitigate mali-cious overwriting actions and data, alongside regulatory sup-port for authenticating users and authorizing their requests.

## C.10 Proof of Bounded Optimization 

We now prove the second claim from §8 on convergence. 

Proof. Here, we will prove that L1 has a lower bound, but L2

does not, so L2 may diverge infinitely. First, we show L1 is bounded below. In particular, L1 ≥ 0. 

L1 is an expectation of a function of one variable: πθ( ˆρA|x).Since πθ( ˆρA|x) is a probability, it is bounded by the interval 

[0, 1]. On the domain [0, 1], L is clearly constrained to [0, ∞),so the expectation L1 is also constrained to [0, ∞), meaning it is bounded below by 0. Next, we will show L2 has no lower bound. L2 is an expec-tation of a function of πθ( ˆρA|x) and πθ(ρA|x). For all x, fix 

πθ( ˆρA|x). Assume there exists a lower bound b ≤ L2. Choose any πθ(ρA|x) < eb+log (πθ( ˆρA|x)) for each x. Then L2 < b, which is a contradiction, so L2 is unbounded below.
