# Combining Homomorphic Encryption and Differential Privacy in Federated Learning for Model Inspection and Availability 

Ceren Yıldırım , Kamer Kaya , Sinan Yıldırım , and Erkay Savas ¸ , Member, IEEE 

Abstract —The increasing prevalence of decentralized data has led to a growing interest in federated learning, which enables collaborative model training without clients sharing their sensitive local data. However, FL alone does not sufficiently protect sensitive training data and is generally coupled with privacy-preserving techniques, such as differential privacy and homomorphic encryption. Although powerful, these techniques address separate concerns via different mechanisms, so relying on just one might prove insufficient or impractical for addressing challenges associated with federated learning. In this work, we propose a privacy-preserving federated learning framework that combines homomorphic encryption-based training with differ-ential privacy-based model inspection and release. We adopt a Markov chain Monte Carlo-based Bayesian privacy estimation method to estimate the privacy of our proposed framework. Our results show that this method improves both model utility and estimated privacy over the baseline method that relies solely on differential privacy for training. In our experiments with the FEMNIST dataset, by the end of training, our method reaches a test loss of 1.09 , compared to 2.37 for the differential privacy-only approach, while providing stronger estimated privacy protection, with the estimated posterior mean of the privacy parameter ϵ of 

4.32 , compared to 7.26 for the differential privacy-only approach. We also show that intermittent model monitoring can preserve the encrypted training trajectory while, under our evaluated experimental setting, providing estimated privacy comparable to or stronger than the differential privacy-only approach. 

Index Terms —Federated learning, homomorphic encryption, differential privacy, model inspection, privacy-preserving ma-chine learning. 

I. I NTRODUCTION 

# IN recent years, the rapid growth of data generated and stored in a distributed fashion has driven increasing inter-est in Federated Learning (FL), a Machine Learning (ML) paradigm that enables decentralized data owners to collabora-tively train a model without sharing their local data. In FL, each data owner trains a model locally using their confidential data and shares the local model updates with a server. The server aggregates the updates it receives from the clients to produce a global model update, which it redistributes to the clients. This local training and aggregation process repeats over multiple rounds until the model reaches a target accuracy. FL has also been widely recognized as a privacy-enhancing solution as it ensures each client’s sensitive data remains within its own local environment. However, recent studies have shown that local and global model updates can still reveal information about confidential training data [1]–[3]. Homomorphic Encryption (HE) and Differential Privacy (DP) have become common approaches for developing privacy-preserving FL systems [4], [5]. While both DP and HE are powerful tools for enhancing privacy in FL, they are not interchangeable, as their different underlying mechanisms make them better suited to address different privacy concerns. Relying solely on either method is insufficient to ensure training data confidentiality while pre-serving the utility of the resulting model. DP protects sensitive training data by injecting noise into model updates during FL. However, estimating the privacy of complex ML models and calibrating noise accordingly are challenging tasks. Moreover, relying on often overly conservative worst-case theoretical bounds can significantly reduce model utility, especially when strong privacy guarantees are required. HE allows computations to be performed directly on en-crypted model updates. Although it also introduces a small amount of noise during encryption, this noise differs funda-mentally from the noise deliberately added by DP to achieve privacy guarantees. In HE, noise is merely a byproduct of en-cryption and subsequent operations, and is typically negligible in practice. This greatly reduces the utility degradation often associated with DP, and information remains protected as long as it remains encrypted. However, once sensitive information or its outputs are decrypted, they are no longer protected by HE. For instance, the final model or its inference results released in plaintext may still reveal information about the underlying training data. Therefore, additional mechanisms, such as DP, are required for privacy-preserving downstream use. Moreover, fully encrypted training complicates monitoring the learning process. For instance, assessing model conver-gence, deciding when to halt the protocol, or detecting utility-harming client behavior becomes non-trivial when model updates are encrypted. Even non-malicious clients with low-quality or biased data may inadvertently degrade global model quality. Identifying such cases during training is important to ensure that the final model remains useful. However, if the entire training process is performed under HE, these checks must also be carried out over encrypted information, making it much more difficult to inspect the progression of model training privately. In this work, we propose a privacy-preserving FL protocol that combines HE and DP to leverage the respective advan-tages of both techniques. We use HE to preserve model utility during federated training, while employing DP for intermittent model inspection and privacy-preserving downstream release. The entire federated training workflow operates on encrypted and non-perturbed model updates, and DP is applied only to arXiv:2610.01650v1 [cs.CR] 1 Oct 2026 2

separate copies of the global updates that are subsequently made publicly available for inspection or downstream use. As noted, estimating the privacy of complex ML models and determining appropriate noise levels accordingly remain challenging problems. To address this, we use a Markov chain Monte Carlo (MCMC)-based Bayesian estimation method for DP based on Membership Inference Attacks (MIAs), intro-duced in [6]. We adapt this method to assess privacy in FL by introducing a new attack definition and corresponding test statistics. This lets us estimate the full posterior distribution of the privacy parameters, accounting for uncertainty in attack performance and avoiding overly confident privacy estimates. In our evaluation, we compare three FL scenarios that represent different privacy-utility trade-offs. These include 1) a DP-only baseline scenario where noise is injected into the local model updates before each aggregation round, 2) a scenario where training is fully achieved using HE and DP is applied to separate copies of model updates for inspection and release of the final model, 3) and a scenario where training is fully achieved using HE and DP is applied only to the final model before release. Our findings indicate that the third setting yields lower estimated privacy loss than the first while providing better utility. Furthermore, we show that intermittent monitoring (i.e., the second setting) can be achieved without perturbing or modifying the encrypted training process, while still providing lower estimated privacy loss than that of the first setting.

A. Federated Learning 

Federated Learning (FL) is a technique that enables multiple clients to collaboratively train an ML model while keeping their local data concealed from other participants. This tech-nique ensures that sensitive training data is never directly shared with any third parties. Instead, each client trains an ML model on its local data and shares only the gradients or parameters of the local update with a central server (hereafter referred to as the server). These shared model parameters are combined to produce a global model update. The FL process continues for multiple rounds until the global model achieves a target accuracy. The most widely employed technique for FL is federated averaging (FedAvg) [7]. A training cohort T of size K begins the protocol with a randomly initialized global model. B. Homomorphic Encryption 

Homomorphic Encryption (HE) is a cryptographic tech-nique that allows computation on encrypted data without requiring the decryption key. The general idea can be ex-pressed as Enc( m1) ⊗ Enc( m2) = Enc( m1 ◦ m2), where Enc 3

represents the encryption operation, ⊗ denotes an operation in the encrypted domain, and ◦ is an operation, e.g., addi-tion or multiplication, in the plaintext domain. HE schemes are categorized by their computational capabilities and the complexity of functions they can evaluate. Among these, Fully Homomorphic Encryption (FHE) stands out as it can support any computable function and is not restricted by the number of consecutive operations. This is achieved through a process called bootstrapping [4]. Bootstrapping refreshes the ciphertext by homomorphically reducing the noise that accumulates with each applied homomorphic operation, thus enabling an unlimited number of operations. Some of the most popular FHE schemes include the Cheon-Kim-Kim-Song (CKKS) [11], Brakerski/Fan-Vercauteren (BFV) [12], and Fast Fully Homomorphic En-cryption over the Torus (TFHE) [13] schemes. CKKS supports approximate arithmetic over fixed-point and complex numbers, BFV supports exact arithmetic over integers, and TFHE is optimized for gate-level Boolean logic and arithmetic over bits. Because it supports fixed-point numbers, CKKS is generally preferred for machine learning applications. The security of these schemes is based on the hardness assumption of the Ring Learning with Errors (RLWE) problem and provides semantic security (IND-CPA). Furthermore, BFV and CKKS schemes also support batching , an encoding technique that packs multiple plaintext values into a single ciphertext. This enables Single-Instruction, Multiple-Data (SIMD) operations, significantly improving the computational throughput. 

C. Differential Privacy 

Differential Privacy (DP) is a mathematical framework that aims to prevent an individual’s information from being inferred through an algorithm’s output. This is generally achieved by adding noise to the computational process. The noise level is carefully calibrated to strike a balance between privacy and utility. Excessive noise can disrupt the statistical properties of the output, while minimal noise may not provide sufficient privacy. Therefore, the noise needs to be just large enough so that the output distributions from two neighboring datasets are nearly indistinguishable. The neighborhood relation depends on the context. Typically, two datasets are neighbors if one dataset can be obtained by adding/removing (or replacing) one individual’s data from the other dataset. 

Definition 1. A randomized mechanism M : U 7 → V is (ϵ, δ )-DP if for all neighboring (D, D′) pairs and all V ∈ V ,

P(M(D) ∈ V ) ≤ eϵ P(M(D′) ∈ V ) + δ. 

1) Estimating DP: Although DP serves as a powerful tool for safeguarding the privacy of individual data, estimating the privacy parameters (ϵ, δ ) remains challenging. This is especially true in complex scenarios that involve multiple randomized operations, such as training sophisticated ML models. In these cases, a considerable gap often exists between theoretical guarantees and the privacy actually achieved by the model. As a result, there has been growing interest in empirical estimation of these parameters. Empirical methodologies often leverage the relationship between DP and membership inference attacks (MIAs) to estimate privacy. An MIA can be formulated as a statistical test in which the attacker attempts to determine whether a target sample or individual was included in a target model’s training data. Various studies in the literature employ either frequentist or Bayesian frameworks for privacy estimation. For instance, [14], [15] use Clopper-Pearson confidence intervals, while [6], [16] rely on Bayesian credible intervals to provide estimates for privacy parameters. The effectiveness of the underlying attack is critical to privacy estimation. While the likelihood ratio test (LRT) is the most powerful attack in principle, its direct practical application is not feasible, leading MIAs to rely on various approximations. These approximations include LOSS [17], a loss-based test that is effective in identifying non-membership but performs poorly in detecting membership, and LiRa [18], which provides a more direct approximation of the LRT. III. R ELATED WORK 

Several works in the literature aim to provide privacy in FL using various techniques, including differential privacy (DP), homomorphic encryption (HE), secure multi-party com-putation (SMPC), trusted execution environments (TEEs), and decentralization [19]. In this section, we focus primarily on DP and HE, as our work uses these two methods to achieve privacy. 

a) Differential Privacy: There are two main types of DP in FL: Centralized Differential Privacy (CDP) and Local Dif-ferential Privacy (LDP). In CDP, the server perturbs the global model update after aggregating the local models received from the clients [20]. Existing works that use CDP in the domain of FL include [21]. This approach is applicable when the server can be trusted, an assumption that does not hold in most real-world scenarios. In LDP, by contrast, each client perturbs their local information themselves before transmitting it to the server [22]. Existing works that use LDP in the domain of FL include [23], [24]. Although LDP protects against an untrusted server, ensuring privacy guarantees while maintaining model utility becomes a major challenge as model size increases. This is due to the privacy budget being spread over a large parameter space, which necessitates adding more noise to the model [25]. 

b) Homomorphic Encryption: Several works utilize HE to achieve varying degrees of privacy. In [26], an improved version of the Paillier cryptosystem is used. In this work, all clients share the same single secret/public key pair at each round. However, this method is vulnerable to collusion attacks between the server and any client, as well as infiltration attacks, due to the secret key being shared. In [27], the authors use multi-key homomorphic encryption over the CKKS encryption scheme (xMK-CKKS). In this scheme, the secret key is divided into multiple shares, with each share owned by a different client. This approach ensures that all clients must collaborate to decrypt any ciphertext, improving upon the security guarantees of [26]. While this protocol secures local model weights, the global model re-mains visible to the server and is not collusion-resistant when 4

K − 1 clients collude, where K is the total number of clients involved in the protocol. Moreover, in both [26] and [27], the clients can access the global update in plaintext, making the model susceptible to statistical attacks. One of the most notable works in the literature in terms of privacy protection is the P OSEIDON framework [28]. For privacy protection, this framework uses multiparty homomor-phic encryption (MHE), following a key-distribution structure similar to xMK-CKKS. In this framework, training is con-ducted entirely homomorphically, and model updates remain encrypted at all times. After a certain number of communi-cation rounds, the model can either be decrypted for further analysis or kept confidential for encrypted model inference. The protocol provides end-to-end model protection; however, it has some limitations:  

> •

Keeping the model encrypted throughout the FL process makes it infeasible to inspect its utility during training. This complicates decisions about when to stop training and makes it difficult to detect anomalies during training.  

> •

To utilize the trained model in downstream tasks, the authors suggest perturbing the model’s output (e.g., predictions) with DP. However, this method results in some privacy loss with each query, which accumulates over time and limits the number of queries the model can effectively process. Our proposed method is specifically designed to address these two critical limitations of the P OSEIDON protocol. IV. B. Limitations of Privacy-Preserving FL 

In P OSEIDON , output perturbation is suggested for provid-ing DP. When a query is made for a selected input, inference is performed homomorphically over the encrypted model. The resulting encrypted prediction is then perturbed with DP noise, after which the noisy prediction is decrypted and released to the querier. However, the privacy loss associated with this approach accumulates with the number of queries due to the 

sequential composition property of DP, limiting how many times the final model can be used privately.

A serious challenge within the model perturbation approach is deriving tight theoretical privacy bounds in complex ML pipelines. Overly conservative theoretical bounds may lead to unnecessarily large noise, resulting in perturbed models with very low utility. To mitigate this, we propose empirical privacy auditing to estimate the model’s privacy at a given noise level, and ultimately determine the noise level based on those estimates. Privacy auditing is based on designing membership inference attacks (MIA) tailored to the DP defini-tion, measuring the performance of MIA on several instances, and estimating DP parameters from the performance results. Several methods have been proposed for this purpose [6], [16]. In this work, we adopt the Bayesian privacy estimation algorithm in [6] and extend it to this work by adapting the attack definition and test statistic to the FL setting. Another shortcoming of executing the full training pipeline homomorphically is that it effectively turns the model up-dates and any intermediate values into a “ black box ”. This makes it significantly more challenging to determine when the model has reached a satisfactory level of utility and when to stop training. Furthermore, it becomes difficult to identify anomalies that could potentially harm the training process. Clients may provide low-quality, imbalanced, or bi-ased datasets, which can negatively affect the model without 5

them realizing it. Therefore, it is essential to conduct model inspections during training. One way to conduct inspections is to examine the global model updates after specific rounds. By accessing plaintext global model updates, clients can run comprehensive statistical tests or evaluate the model’s classification performance on a separate test set. This method avoids additional computa-tional and communication costs associated with HE during inspections. However, making global model updates publicly available poses a risk of privacy breaches. A better alternative is to conduct the privacy-preserving inspections on perturbed copies of these model updates during specific rounds using DP. Since the noisy model is merely a temporary copy and the training continues with the non-noisy version, the added noise will not affect the accuracy of the model being trained (see Fig. 1(b)).

D. DP Estimation for FL 

To estimate the privacy guarantees of our proposed FL pro-tocol, we use the MCMC-based Bayesian estimation technique introduced in [6]. Our choice of this method is motivated by its ability to estimate the full posterior distribution of the privacy parameters, rather than merely relying on credible intervals. It also consolidates evidence from multiple attacks, leading to a more robust privacy estimate. Moreover, it does not assume that any single attack is the strongest attack, or that the attack is performed on the worst-case challenge base . Instead, it parametrizes and estimates the average strength of the applied attacks and challenge bases. Finally, the method provides an efficient approach for evaluating attack performance and can accommodate statistical dependencies, as it naturally extends to joint probability models. The estimation procedure can be represented as a hierar-chical model over (ϵ, s ) parameters, denoting the privacy and attack strength. Ideally, the attack’s true type I (false positive) and type II (false negative) error probabilities are used as the input to the privacy estimation algorithm; however, these are latent values that can only be estimated via error counts obtained through multiple attack executions. The auditing procedure can be outlined in three main steps:  

> •

Designing the attack: [6] is based on a parametric version of LiRA [18], which is a loss-based attack. For privacy estimation, the choice of the test statistic is crucial and depends on the use case and attack definition. Since the FL setting considered in this work differs from the original setting, we introduce a new attack definition and adapt the test statistic accordingly. The resulting attack formulation is described in Section IV-E.  

> •

Measuring attack performance and obtaining error counts: 

To audit the privacy of an algorithm, the attack must be repeated multiple times to estimate the type I and type II error probabilities.  

> •

Estimating privacy from the error counts: Given the esti-mated error probabilities, we estimate the privacy parameters 6

of the algorithm using MCMC-DP-Est , the principal algo-rithm proposed in [6]. This algorithm uses MCMC sampling to estimate the joint posterior distribution of the privacy parameters. 

E. Threat Model and Attack Definition 

In our threat model, all involved participants (i.e., all members of the population P and the server) are assumed to be honest-but-curious. In other words, while they strictly follow the protocol rules, they may attempt to infer private client information from any data available to them. The protocol is resilient to collusion among up to |P|− 1 clients, excluding the server. This choice is due to the low likelihood of a server col-luding in practical deployments, as they are typically operated by large institutions and are heavily regulated. Nevertheless, in Appendix A, we extend this threat model to the setting where the server may collude (with clients). Our threat model characterizes an adversary as either a participant in the protocol (such as a client or the server) or an external entity that can observe the protocol’s actions and outputs. When estimating the privacy of our protocol, we consider the strongest adversarial view , denoted as View in the paper. We compare the information each adversary type can access about the honest client(s)’ data to determine the most revealing view, which we then use for privacy estimation. In any given round r, a group of |P| − 1 colluding clients can share their training membership information among themselves (i.e., whether or not they were included in C(r)). Given that the clients also know |C (r)|, this collaboration can ultimately reveal the honest client’s identity to the colluding clients. Because of this, standard client MIAs are not appropri-ate for assessing the protocol’s privacy, since the membership information is already inferable within our threat model. To address this, we propose an MIA attack tailored for DP, which estimates the privacy of our protocol by attempting to distinguish the honest client’s dataset between two different alternatives. To formalize the MIA definition tailored for DP and assess the protocol’s privacy leakage, we first introduce a challenge base .

Definition 4 (Challenge base) . A challenge base refers to a tuple (D, Z0, Z1), where D is a training dataset of |D| = K−1

clients, and Z0 and Z1 are the two alternative values of the target client’s data. Using the definition for a challenge base, we define MIA tailored for DP as follows. 

Definition 5 (MIA tailored for DP) . Given a challenge base 

(D, Z0, Z1) and an algorithm A, MIA is a statistical test that aims to determine which of two candidate datasets, Z0 or Z1,was used by the target client, by attempting to distinguish between two distributions constructed from the outputs of A

under two different hypotheses: 

H0 : θ ∼ A (D ∪ Z 0), H1 : θ ∼ A (D ∪ Z 1).

The MIA in Definition 5 has two alternative interpretations.  

> •

The first interpretation is related to inferring the target client’s data itself. All |P| − 1 clients’ data are known (e.g., due to collusion among those clients), and the adversary’s intent is to infer the target client’s unknown data. The attack in Definition 5 aims to do that when the possible values of the target client’s data are reduced to two alternatives, Z0

and Z1. In that way, Definition 5 is different from standard MIA in the literature, where one’s membership is inferred. However, this definition is particularly suited to the classical DP setting because it tests the distinguishability of adjacent inputs that differ only in a single client’s contribution.  

> •

A second interpretation may still be made in terms of actual membership inference. Assume the worst-case scenario, usually adopted in MIA settings, that the data of the whole population, {Z c : c ∈ P} , is known. Assume that the population of clients is partitioned into colluding and non-colluding sets of clients as P = PC ∪ P NC , where there is strictly more than one non-colluding client, i.e., |P NC | > 1.Then, a worst-case challenge base in Definition 4 can be constructed where D = {Z c : c ∈ B} , where B ⊆ P C,contains only colluding clients, and Z0 = Zc0 , Z1 = Zc1

where c0, c 1 ∈ P NC are two non-colluding clients. In other words, the training dataset contains K − 1 colluding clients and 1 non-colluding target client. In this case, the attack in Definition 5 can indeed be interpreted as inferring whether the target client is c0 or c1, which is a genuine membership inference attack. Note that having both c0 and c1 in PNC 

is sensible because the membership/non-membership infor-mation of all the colluding clients is already known by the attacker.

A. Dataset 

In our experiments, we use the Federated Extended MNIST (FEMNIST) dataset from LEAF [32]. LEAF is a benchmark-ing framework specifically designed for FL settings, providing open-source datasets constructed to reflect the characteristics of federated environments realistically. Similarly, in the FEM-NIST dataset, the data is naturally partitioned across clients in a heterogeneous fashion, and the number of samples assigned to each client differs considerably. This setup captures the non-i.i.d. and unbalanced data distribution typically observed in practical FL scenarios. We use a random subset of the FEMNIST dataset as the population P. This subset consists of 188 distinct clients (handwriters), each contributing handwriting samples across a subset of 62 distinct characters. Each individual’s data is split into training and test subsets with a training ratio of 0.8.

B. FL Setup 

In the established privacy scenario and proposed FL setting, we select a training cohort T of 100 participants from the population P for model training. In each round r of FL, the server randomly selects a subset of the clients C(r) ⊆ T of size 

Ks = 5 and only the local updates of the selected clients are 9

used to generate the global update of that round. The number of global FL rounds, R, is set to 500 , and the number of local epochs is set to 1. During local training, the batch size is set to 16 . We use Stochastic Gradient Descent (SGD) as the optimizer with a momentum of 0.9. We set the learning rate, μ, to 0.01 and apply label smoothing with a coefficient of 0.01 to stabilize training. The same smoothed loss is also utilized when reporting the test loss for utility evaluation in Section V. The model we use is a lightweight convolutional neural network (CNN). C. Attack Evaluation 1) Challenge Construction: In our experiments, we gener-ate a total of n = 20 challenge bases, and each challenge base is constructed such that Di ∪ Z i, 0 and Di ∪ Z i, 1 includes 100 

individuals’ data, where i ∈ { 1, . . . , n }. For each challenge base, we train N shadow models under each hypothesis. To measure the attack performance, we use the leave-one-out technique (see MeasureMIAFast in [6]). In our experiments, the unit noise generated by clients (and added to the local models) in A1 is zero-mean Gaussian noise with a fixed standard deviation of σ = 0 .02 , unless specified otherwise. For the algorithms A2/3, the standard deviation of the noise the server adds to the global models is determined based on the derivations discussed in Section IV-E2, and it varies depending on the number of data points held by each client in the subset c ∈ C (r). Although the variance of the noise introduced to the global model update at each round remains consistent across all algorithms ( A1/2/3), it can differ from one round to the next. We present the results starting from round 100 , as earlier rounds do not provide reliable privacy estimates due to our attack not being the strongest or most ideal. 

D. Results 1) Privacy and Effects on Utility: One of our main claims is that A3 can achieve higher estimated privacy than A1 while maintaining the same or improved levels of utility. We utilize our MCMC-based privacy estimation procedure to calculate the posterior mean of ϵ, focusing on the most informative models for the target client that an adversary can extract from the protocol outputs available in its view. For A3, we report the estimated privacy at each round by treating the model at that round as the final released model. In other words, each point on the x-axis corresponds to the privacy estimate if training were terminated and the DP-perturbed model were released at that round. For utility evaluations, we select a single challenge base under H0 and report the average test loss across its N = 100 

associated shadow models evaluated on the concatenated local test sets of the clients in H0, since the full set of challenge bases and hypotheses is required only for privacy estimation. As shown in Fig. 2(a), at round 500 , for comparable levels of utility, corresponding to test losses of 1.00 for A1 and 0.86 

for A3, the estimated privacy loss of A1 is noticeably higher than that of A3, with posterior mean estimates of ϵ of 9.13 and 

6.02 , respectively. This trend persists throughout the remainder of the training process, supporting our argument that training under HE and applying DP only at model release yields lower estimated privacy loss at comparable utility than perturbing the global update each round and continuing training on the perturbed model. Moreover, Fig. 2(b) demonstrates that even at higher noise levels (when required to achieve stronger pri-vacy), at round 500 , A3 can attain better utility than A1, with losses of 1.09 and 2.37 , respectively, while still preserving a large improvement in estimated privacy, with posterior mean estimates of ϵ of 4.32 and 7.26 , respectively. This trend again persists throughout the remainder of the training process. While results in Fig. 2 support our claim that the final model can be released privately for downstream use while preserving privacy and accuracy through HE-based training, we also need to evaluate whether intermittent model inspection in meaningful periods can be performed without incurring greater privacy leakage than the DP-only baseline. To this end, Fig. 3 reports the estimated posterior mean of ϵ across training rounds for different values of P , where P denotes the inspection period. For example, when P = 10 , we inspect every 10th model until all 500 training rounds are complete. In our experiments, the results show that even when P = 1 

(i.e., every global model is subjected to inspection), A2

consistently achieves a lower posterior mean of ϵ compared to A1 over the training rounds. At the final round (i.e., round 

500 ), the estimated privacy loss for A2 reaches 8.43 , compared to 9.13 for A1. This indicates that our framework preserves model utility through HE-based training while still enabling frequent inspections. These results also show that increasing the inspection period improves protocol privacy, which may be preferable for particularly sensitive tasks that do not require as rigorous inspections. It is also important to note that the estimated privacy loss of the protocols is dependent on the variance of noise added, the model architecture, the FL setup, and the properties of the data. Thus, while A2 incurs less privacy loss than A1, even for P = 1 in our setting, this may not necessarily be the case under other experimental configurations. Our privacy estimation procedure requires training N × n shadow models for each hypothesis, resulting in 4, 000 FL runs in our exper-imental setting. Training this many models already imposes substantial computational overhead in the cleartext domain. Since homomorphic operations can be orders of magnitude more expensive than cleartext operations, performing fully HE-based training for all shadow models would be compu-tationally impractical for privacy estimation. Nevertheless, it is essential to validate our assumption and demonstrate that training under HE results in a negligible difference in model utility, thus supporting the claims made in Section V-D1. The difference between cleartext and HE can primarily arise from two factors:  

> •

Nonlinear operations , such as the ReLU and softmax func-tions used in our model, cannot be applied directly in a homomorphic setting and must be replaced with polynomial or arithmetic approximations.  

> •

CKKS is an approximate encryption scheme . Therefore, a small amount of numerical noise is introduced into the un-derlying plaintexts during encrypted arithmetic operations. These factors may cause the model to follow a different optimization trajectory, potentially leading to different ac-curacy and loss values. To ensure that the effect of HE-induced numerical noise and nonlinear approximation remains within an acceptable range, we implemented a simulated FHE version of the FL pipeline for A2/3, using the Alternating Packing (AP) mechanism introduced in P OSEIDON [28]. In this implementation, we approximate the ReLU function using the representation ReLU (x) = x(1 + sgn( x)) /2, where sgn( ·)

denotes the sign function and is polynomially approximated using the composite method in [33]. We approximate the soft-max function using a Taylor series expansion, Goldschmidt di-vision, and the maximum-finding algorithm presented in [33]. Moreover, we introduce estimated CKKS numerical noise into each simulated homomorphic operation using Tune In-sight’s CKKS noise estimation library [34]. This library rep-resents each ciphertext by mock ciphertext components in canonical form and propagates the noisy components through the circuit operation by operation. Furthermore, we adapt a similar noise estimation mechanism for the DBootstrap 

and DBootstrapALT operations, which are not available in the original library, by comparing the generated noise in our simulated outputs with that produced by the GPU-accelerated FHE library, HEonGPU [35]. Fig. 5 compares the test loss of the cleartext alternative of

In this work, we proposed a utility-preserving FL protocol that enables privacy-preserving model inspection and down-stream model release by integrating HE and DP. Our experi-ments demonstrate that our method offers significantly greater estimated privacy while also providing better utility than the basic approach based solely on DP, which continues training on a previously perturbed model. Consequently, these findings indicate that for use cases requiring higher noise variances to achieve sufficient privacy, our proposed approach can avoid the utility degradation that arises from directly adding DP noise to the model updates used during training. This is because the HE-based approach avoids noise accumulation by only perturbing the final layer of the visible models. Furthermore, we show that model release and intermittent inspections can occur while maintaining estimated privacy levels higher than a strictly DP-based pipeline. These findings are particularly significant because they enable privacy-preserving access to the model during and after training. As future work, the current HE simulation can be replaced with a full homomorphic implementation to investigate and improve the computational and communication costs of the proposed protocol. In particular, arithmetic and polynomial approximations of the involved nonlinear functions could be optimized to incur less computational overhead in a real-world implementation. Further research could also examine the impact of non-periodic inspections on privacy, as participants may find it more useful to review the model closer to the anticipated end of the training protocol, rather than having fre-quent inspections at the beginning. Other potential directions 12 

include extending our framework to definition (a) by adapting the null and alternative hypotheses accordingly, and exploring various methods for utilizing intermittent models in anomaly detection. ACKNOWLEDGMENT

APPENDIX AEXTENDED THREAT MODEL 

In our threat model presented in Section IV-E, we assume that the server cannot collude, even though the clients can. We specifically opted for this assumption for the server, as in large-scale federated learning applications, the servers tend to be operated by large organizations whose behavior and actions are heavily regulated compared to individual, possibly anonymous clients. Nevertheless, our threat model and protocol can be extended to the setting where the server may collude (with clients). In this section, we define an extended version of our threat model outlined in Section IV-E, which accounts for both the clients and the server colluding as semi-honest entities. We refer to the threat model described in Section IV-E as T1, and our extended model as T2.

A. A1 (DP-only baseline) 

Both the FL algorithm and the adversary’s view (collective view of all colluding parties, i.e., the server and |P|− 1 clients) are the same as in T1 (refer to Alg. 1 and Eq. 2, respectively). Therefore, A1 provides the same privacy guarantees in T2 as it does in T1. Its privacy can be estimated using the attack definition outlined in Section IV-E3. The only distinction is that, since the server is responsible for selecting clients each round for training ( C(r) $

← T , ∀r) and informing them, the collective can now possess the knowledge of the honest clients’ identities directly. This removes the standard membership in-ference implications associated with our MIA in Definition 5.
